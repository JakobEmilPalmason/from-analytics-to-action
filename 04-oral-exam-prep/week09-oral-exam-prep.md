# Week 9 — Resistance in the Data-Driven Society: Oral Exam Prep

## 1. Week overview

This week pairs two complementary readings on the *discontents* of datafication: one from outside the firm, one from inside. Milan (2024) catalogues how individuals and collectives push back against datafied infrastructures, distinguishing *defensive* resistance (self-defence, subversion, avoidance) from *productive* resistance (literacy, counter-imagination, advocacy campaigning). Hoeyer and Wadmann (2020), studying the highly digitalized Danish healthcare sector, show how clinicians come to experience their own data work as "meaningless" — a "yoke of Kafkaesque idiocy" (Jacobsen, 2018, in Hoeyer & Wadmann, p. 434) that erodes professional judgement even as no one in the chain feels able to stop the data mill. The two papers operate at different scales — Milan globally and politically, Hoeyer and Wadmann at the ward level — but both treat datafication as something that gets *contested* in practice, whether overtly (a Hong Kong protester pulling down a smart lamppost, Milan, p. 2) or covertly (a quality coordinator who knows the staff fill in pressure-ulcer screenings before seeing the patient, Hoeyer & Wadmann, p. 439). The practical pay-off for the case work is dual: students should be able to (i) name where resistance to a case dataset is likely to occur and what tactic it will take, and (ii) read complaints about "meaningless work" as a diagnostic signal that the data system has come uncoupled from organizational goal orientation.

## 2. Key concepts

- **Resistance** — "intentional actions taken in opposition to someone or something, arising from a refusal to conform to social norms or accept the status quo (or changes to it)" (Hollander & Einwohner, 2004, in Milan, 2024, p. 2; quoted lecture slide 5).
- **Defensive vs. productive resistance** — Milan's (2024, p. 7) two macro-categories, drawing on Ettlinger (2018): defensive tactics target the socio-technical infrastructure of datafication; productive tactics target its symbolic level (norms, values, narratives).
- **Self-defence** — adopting "software tools to bypass surveillance, state snooping, and the data exploitation perpetrated by tech companies" plus "behavioural techniques of neutralising surveillance such as masking or blocking" (Milan, p. 8, drawing on Marx, 2003).
- **Subversion** — destabilising data-driven structures via sabotage, whistleblowing, or "data obfuscation" — putting "sand in the machine" (Milan, p. 8). Brunton and Nissenbaum's (2013, p. 171) obfuscation is "a form of redress in the absence of any other protection and defence, and one which disproportionately aids the weak against the strong."
- **Avoidance** — "rejecting digital life or parts thereof" (Ettlinger, 2018, p. 5, in Milan, p. 9): boycotts, digital disconnection, alternative platforms. Milan flags it as increasingly "impractical" given mandatory smartphone-based welfare and identity services (p. 12; lecture slide 8).
- **Literacy** — "teaching and learning how to protect individual liberties and retain a certain degree of opacity in times of data-driven surveillance" (Milan, p. 10), e.g. Cryptoparties / Privacy Cafés (Monsees, 2020).
- **Counter-imagination** — "narrative intervention that seeks to create alternative narratives that challenge dominant discourses and/or to flip the perspective on problematic aspects of datafication" (Kazansky & Milan, 2021, in Milan, p. 10).
- **Advocacy campaigning** — concerted effort to "ask for better rules and laws, or for the suspensions of industry and/or state practices" (Milan, p. 11), e.g. Reclaim Your Face, Ban the Scan.
- **Three transformations of resistance** — Milan's (p. 6) account of how datafication reshapes resistance along *infrastructure* (digitalised public sphere, intrusive surveillance), *political agency* (algorithmically mediated sense-making), and *tactics* (incorporation of digital and datafied elements).
- **Anticipatory data practices** — Kazansky's (2021) idea, cited in Milan (p. 8), that resistance is increasingly future-oriented: activists "play ahead" to pre-empt harms.
- **Surveillance realism** — Dencik's (2018) term (Milan, p. 12) for the dominant assumption that pervasive data extraction is inevitable, against which productive resistance must work.
- **Data assemblage** — Kitchin's (2014) term, central to Hoeyer and Wadmann (p. 435): "socio-technical systems comprising not just a given data archive or repository but also the technological, political, social and economic apparatuses that shape how these systems operate."
- **'Meaningless work'** — empirically grounded category (Hoeyer & Wadmann, p. 435) for tasks that staff perceive as disconnected from clinical goals: "data massage", "fiddling", "to keep the system silent" (p. 439).
- **Data as symbol vs. as decision-support** — drawing on Feldman and March (1981), Hoeyer and Wadmann argue clinicians "value data as symbols" rather than "decision-support tools" (p. 447); data become a medium of inter-organizational recognition.
- **Self-referential data / dissolved referentiality** — Poster (1990) via Hoeyer and Wadmann (p. 446): in saturated data environments "what is real to the organization is what is in the data," and referentiality (the link between data and the world) "dissolves again."
- **Logic of escalation** — Pollitt et al. (2010), in Hoeyer and Wadmann (p. 442): once data are required to "build arguments," every meaningful claim demands more data, so reform initiatives generate further data work rather than reducing it.
- **Campbell's Law** — "the more any quantitative social indicator is used for social decision-making, the more subject it will be to corruption pressures and the more apt it will be to distort and corrupt the social processes it is intended to monitor" (Campbell, 1979, p. 85, in Hoeyer & Wadmann, p. 442).
- **Cover My Ass (DDR) culture** — clinical-staff vernacular reported by Hoeyer and Wadmann (p. 446) for a defensive documentation regime where the physician's attention shifts "from what is good for the patient to the physician's own ass" (Lange, 2017, in H&W p. 447).

## 3. Per-paper notes

### Milan, S. (2024), "Resistance in the data-driven society," *Internet Policy Review* 13(4)

**(a) Core argument.** Datafication, coupled with the rise of intelligent systems, has "fundamentally reshaped the possibilities for resistance" (p. 3). The essay does three things: it situates contemporary resistance in a longer history of contentious politics; it identifies three datafication-driven transformations (infrastructure, political agency, tactics); and it maps six tactics, grouped into defensive (self-defence, subversion, avoidance) and productive (literacy, counter-imagination, advocacy campaigning) resistance. Looking at resistance *to* datafication, Milan argues, illuminates how resistance more broadly will have to evolve to "survive the test of time" (p. 12).

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Conceptual essay drawing on examples from across the globe — Hong Kong's smart-lampposts protest, Snowden, #DeleteFacebook, Reclaim Your Face, Ban the Scan, Chupadados, CV Dazzle, Qubes, Go Rando — and a literature review across sociology, cultural studies, gender/queer studies, postcolonial/decolonial studies, and media studies. No new empirics; the contribution is taxonomic and theoretical.

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- On the central question: "how are resistance practices adapting and evolving in today's increasingly data-driven and digitalised society?" (p. 2).
- On the unevenness of digital resistance: "occasions to connect to bystanders have multiplied, having removed the bottleneck of traditional media outlets... On the other hand, messages proliferate, audiences are more diffused than ever, and digital commitment does not always translate into embodied action, undermining the sustainability of resistance itself" (p. 6).
- On data obfuscation: it is "a form of redress in the absence of any other protection and defence, and one which disproportionately aids the weak against the strong" (Brunton & Nissenbaum, 2013, p. 171, in Milan p. 8).
- On the limits of avoidance: "the likely impracticality of certain defensive resistance tactics, such as escapist approaches, due to the growing reliance on smartphone apps for accessing essential services like welfare and identity verification, rendering these strategies increasingly unsustainable" (p. 12).
- On counter-imagination's risk: such projects can promote an "aestheticization of resistance premised on individual avoidance rather than meaningful challenge to the violent and discriminatory logics of surveillance societies" (Monahan, 2015, p. 159, in Milan p. 11).

**(d) Connection to the week.** Provides the *vocabulary*. The six-tactic typology is the week's main analytic instrument: every resistance phenomenon in the lecture's examples (FoodCoach non-uses, MyData, Hestia Labs and Uber drivers in Geneva) can be mapped onto one or more tactics, and the lecture explicitly imports the defensive/productive split (slide 8). Crucially, Milan also names *why* these tactics matter for analysts: by degrading data quality, resistance signals "misalignment between system and reality/values" (lecture slide 6, building on Milan).

### Hoeyer, K. & Wadmann, S. (2020), "'Meaningless work': How the datafication of health reconfigures knowledge about work and erodes professional judgement," *Economy and Society* 49(3)

**(a) Core argument.** Datafication and digitalization in Danish healthcare have produced a *data assemblage* in which "data can operate as symbols of knowledge (rather than pathways to knowledge)" (p. 435). Because local data are made visible upward to people who cannot assess clinical value, staff invest meaning and effort in data they do not believe in — and the result is "meaningless work" with three implications: financial cost, epistemic doubt, and a changed moral landscape that erodes goal orientation and professional judgement. The paper explicitly refuses the bifurcation between rational data and irrational complaints: "there is no ontological distinction between meaningless and meaningful work" (p. 435).

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Ongoing fieldwork in the Danish healthcare sector since 2015: interviews with 74 people across state, region, and municipality (data analysts, hospital and clinical-department managers, nurses, physicians, secretaries, audit workers, patient activists), four days of ward observation plus a management seminar, plus policy-document analysis (p. 438). Material was first coded for meaningful/meaningless and then thematically recoded when the distinction failed to hold.

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- The diagnostic claim: "experiences of meaningless work partly result from technologically mediated ways of making sense of work" (p. 435).
- The vernacular: tasks "to keep the system silent", described with "ironic gestures, jeers and raised eyebrows"; one GP said "it makes no sense" forty-six times in a single interview (p. 439).
- The structural mechanism: "In a form of bureaucracy-on-steroids where only data-based forms of reasoning count, it is with data that the staff pursue meaningful action even when it implies doing 'meaningless work' in a strictly clinical sense... '[Data collection] is the way we build arguments, it is the way we work'" (quality monitor, p. 441).
- Standardization's loss of validity: "When health professionals no longer recognize standards as valid signifiers of their practice, they also lose legitimacy (moral value). It is typically in these situations that discourses of 'meaningless work' begin proliferating among the clinicians" (p. 442).
- The dissolution of referentiality: "you can hide as many things today as you used to be able to, because you can just produce bad data, invalid data, then the documentation is there. It's just as if a slippery slope has been opened somehow" (quality coordinator, pp. 445–446).
- The closing warning: "Datafication might do most harm when people believe too much in data, and forget to retain room for judgement" (p. 449; quoted lecture slide 23).

**(d) Connection to the week.** Where Milan describes deliberate, often political resistance, Hoeyer and Wadmann show what happens when resistance is *suppressed* or remains only at the level of complaint. Their "data massage", "fiddling", and pre-filled screenings are textbook examples of Milan's *subversion* tactic — Brunton and Nissenbaum's obfuscation under the skin of the organization — but the paper's contribution is to show the *cost* of leaving these covert tactics to do all the work: epistemic doubt, financial waste, and the moral drift toward Cover My Ass culture (p. 446). The paper supplies the operational vocabulary — data assemblage, logic of escalation, data as symbol — for diagnosing meaningless work in the case companies.

## 4. Lecture connections

The lecture (Madsen, 13 April 2026) is organized around three claims that the readings together support. First (slides 5–6), resistance "happens in data collection (what people choose to share), data generation (how they behave because of systems), and system interaction (workarounds, etc.)" — and matters for analysts because it "degrades data quality, produces misleading outputs, and signals misalignment between system and reality/values." This is the productive analytic move: complaints and workarounds are not noise to be filtered out but data about data.

Second (slides 7–8), Milan's article is presented as an evolutionary account: resistance has a long pedigree (Gandhi, Italian partisans, Rosa Parks, Zapatistas — the opening of Milan, p. 2), but datafication "fundamentally reshapes" what resistance can be (Milan, p. 3, quoted slide 7). The defensive/productive split is reproduced verbatim from Milan's section 3, and the lecturer flags avoidance as increasingly "impractical" given essential services that require a smartphone (slide 8) — directly Milan's closing risk (p. 12).

The lecture's three running examples illustrate one tactic each. **FoodCoach** (slides 10–14), Madsen's own project, is staged as a counter-example where resistance is *expected* and designed for: the social-science team studies "uses and non-uses" of dietary tracking apps along the categories *living with, beyond, against and despite of them* (slide 14, citing Kennedy 2018, Lupton 2016, Pink et al. 2017, Hyysalo et al. 2016) — a vocabulary that maps onto Milan's avoidance and "non-cooperation" registers (p. 9). **MyData** (slide 17) is an *advocacy/literacy* example: a global movement to redistribute control of personal data. **Hestia Labs and the Geneva Uber-driver case** (slides 18–19) sit at the intersection of *subversion* and *literacy*: gig workers exercise GDPR data-access rights for "worker-led 'algorithm auditing'" — a deeply Milan-style productive tactic that nonetheless requires expertise, illustrating Milan's "first challenge" (p. 12) about the "level of technical knowledge... required for self-defence."

The Design–Break–Resistance activity on the delivery-driver metric (slides 15–16) is the lecturer's bridge to Hoeyer and Wadmann: it asks students to *predict* meaningless work before the system is built — which data become "useless from a clinical perspective", which become Cover My Ass screenings (p. 446). The "meaningless work" thread (slides 21–24) reproduces Hoeyer and Wadmann's three implications (financial cost, epistemic doubt, changed moral landscape, p. 444) and ends on the central provocation, quoted directly from the paper: "Datafication might do most harm when people believe too much in data and forget to retain room for judgement" (slide 23; H&W p. 449). The closing case prompts (slides 20, 25) ask students to envision resistance and locate "meaningless work" in the Rigshospitalet and Will & Agency cases — i.e. to use the week's two readings as paired diagnostic instruments.

## 5. Cross-paper synthesis

- **Agreement that resistance is a signal, not a nuisance.** Milan treats resistance as politically generative — the *subject* of the analysis. Hoeyer and Wadmann do not use the word "resistance" prominently, but their description of staff "fiddling", "data massage", and quietly fabricating screenings (p. 439) is structurally identical to Milan's everyday subversion / data obfuscation. Both papers refuse to read these acts as failure of compliance: Milan because they are politically intentional (p. 8), Hoeyer and Wadmann because they expose the disconnect between data work and goal orientation (p. 444). The lecture's slogan that "resistance signals misalignment between system and reality/values" (slide 6) is the bridge.
- **Disagreement on register and visibility.** Milan privileges *visible*, often-coordinated tactics with explicit political intent — campaigns, hackathons, projects like Chupadados or CV Dazzle, large NGOs like Amnesty's Ban the Scan (p. 11). Hoeyer and Wadmann work in the opposite direction: their resistance is invisible, individual, frequently unconscious of itself as resistance, and never gets to advocacy campaigning. Milan would call this Scott's "everyday forms of resistance" and "hidden transcripts" (Scott, 1985, 1990, in Milan p. 4); Hoeyer and Wadmann show that when resistance never escapes that register it is *absorbed* by the organization rather than transforming it.
- **Both treat infrastructure as the object that generates the politics.** Milan's first transformation is infrastructural: "the shifting structural conditions of power and inequality... reshaped by the advent of the data-driven society" (p. 6). Hoeyer and Wadmann use Kitchin's data assemblage to make a homologous claim at organizational scale: "these assemblages affect the very perception of what counts as knowledge about work" (p. 435). For both, infrastructure does not merely *transmit* politics; it *constitutes* the field on which politics gets fought.
- **Productive resistance is the bridge.** Milan worries that defensive tactics are increasingly "unsustainable" because of dependence on essential digital services and the inaccessibility of AI even to experts (p. 12), and so privileges *productive* resistance — literacy, counter-imagination, advocacy. Hoeyer and Wadmann, while pessimistic, reach for the same register: their conclusion calls for theorizing "what professional judgement means in data-saturated organizations" (p. 449) — itself a literacy and counter-imagination project, addressed to academics and managers rather than activists.
- **Extension: where the readings diverge most usefully for case work.** Milan's typology is *outward-facing* — it presupposes an adversary (a state, a platform, a surveillance regime) whose data extraction can be openly contested. Hoeyer and Wadmann describe a *no-villain* situation: every actor at every level reports being "merely responding to 'meaningless' demands imposed by external actors" (p. 443), including the state-level civil servant who experiences themselves as responding to others' requests. For case work this is a sharper diagnostic: in many organizations the people who would carry out Milan's productive tactics are *themselves* the ones generating the meaningless work. The two papers together suggest that productive resistance inside an organization will look less like an advocacy campaign and more like a *literacy intervention* aimed at restoring the link between data and clinical (or operational) goal.

## 6. Likely exam questions

**Q1. Distinguish defensive and productive resistance, with examples from Milan.**
Milan (2024, p. 7), drawing on Ettlinger (2018), groups resistance tactics by their target. Defensive tactics — self-defence, subversion, avoidance — engage the *socio-technical infrastructure*: encrypted messaging and Qubes (Kazansky & Milan, 2021, in Milan p. 8); "data obfuscation" tools like Go Rando that "introduce noise in user profiling" (p. 8); and "rejecting digital life or parts thereof" (Ettlinger, 2018, p. 5, in Milan p. 9), as in #DeleteFacebook or the web-2.0-suicide-machine. Productive tactics — literacy, counter-imagination, advocacy campaigning — target *symbolic* aspects: Cryptoparties teach low-skill users to defend their privacy (Monsees, 2020); Chupadados reframes "data-hungry" period-tracking apps (Felizi & Varon, n.d., in Milan p. 11); Reclaim Your Face campaigns for an EU ban on facial recognition. The pay-off of the distinction is normative: defensive tactics buy individual time but, as Milan warns (p. 12), are rendered "increasingly unsustainable" when essential services require a smartphone, so the future of resistance has to be productive.

**Q2. What is "meaningless work" in Hoeyer and Wadmann, and why is it a misleading label?**
Empirically, "meaningless work" is the vernacular Danish clinicians use for data-generation tasks that feel disconnected from clinical care: the "yoke of Kafkaesque idiocy" (Jacobsen, 2018, in H&W p. 434), pre-filling pressure-ulcer screens before seeing patients, recording first symptoms for cancer-pathway audits (p. 443). The label is misleading because Hoeyer and Wadmann argue "there is no ontological distinction between meaningless and meaningful work" (p. 435): what is meaningless at the ward is meaningful at the region, where it documents performance to the state. The right object of analysis is therefore not the tasks themselves but the *data assemblage* (Kitchin, 2014; H&W p. 435) that makes them simultaneously inescapable and clinically inert. The concept's diagnostic value is that it names the moment when staff invest meaning in data they do not believe in, signalling that data have shifted from "decision-support tools" to "symbols" (Feldman & March, 1981, in H&W p. 447).

**Q3. How do Hoeyer and Wadmann describe the dynamics that drive meaningless work, and why do reforms fail to reduce it?**
The driver is what Pollitt et al. (2010) call a "logic of escalation" (H&W p. 442): in an organization where "only data-based forms of reasoning count" (p. 441), even attempts to reduce documentation are themselves data-generating — a quality monitor reports that meetings to ease the documentation burden "typically ended with clinicians requesting new data" so they could "build arguments" (p. 441). Standardization compounds the problem: standards travel from where they were authored to where they fit poorly (the orthopaedic-surgery template applied to neurology, p. 442), and Campbell's Law (1979, p. 85, in H&W p. 442) predicts they will be gamed once they become consequential. Reforms hired to identify removable tasks (Implement, 2009, p. 440) fail because removing data items would render local work invisible at higher levels, and "in an organization where data means visibility, it can be dangerous to rely on 'invisible work'" (Star, 1991, in H&W p. 441). The dynamic is therefore self-reinforcing: more reform → more documentation of reform → more data work.

**Q4. What is the relevance of resistance for data analysts? Use Milan and the lecture.**
Milan reframes resistance as a *signal* rather than as an obstacle. Resistance happens "in data collection (what people choose to share), in data generation (how they behave because of systems), in system interaction (workarounds, etc.)" (lecture slide 6, building on Milan p. 6's discussion of how "any type of contentious action... evolves vis-à-vis the totalising effects of datafication"). For an analyst this is consequential: resistance "degrades data quality, produces misleading outputs, [and] signals misalignment between system and reality/values" (slide 6). The Geneva Uber-driver case (lecture slide 19; Pidoux, Dehaye & Gursky) is illustrative — drivers' GDPR-driven "worker-led 'algorithm auditing'" exposed "discrepancies between actual work and paid work", which is also useful information for the platform's analysts even though it weaponized the data against them. The implication for case work is that any sufficiently rich dataset is partly a record of the system's user resisting it; treating that as bias-to-be-cleaned discards information about where the data are politically vulnerable.

**Q5. Which resistance tactic is most problematic for the value of organizational data, and why?**
*Subversion* is the most directly destructive of organizational data value. Milan (p. 8) describes it as ranging from sabotage and whistleblowing to "foot-dragging techniques of disruption such as polluting data collection to 'put sand in the machine'", with "data obfuscation" (Brunton & Nissenbaum, 2013, p. 171, in Milan p. 8) deliberately corrupting the signal — Go Rando obfuscates Facebook reactions, swapped loyalty cards "muddle the records of supermarket purchases", #DeleteFacebook "exploited [the platform's] popularity mechanisms" (Mills, 2021, p. 852, in Milan p. 9). Hoeyer and Wadmann show the same tactic *inside* an organization: "data massage", pre-filled screenings, fabricated documentation produced because "if you are pressured on something that you can't possibly deliver, you cheat" (p. 445). This dissolves *referentiality* (Poster, 1990, in H&W p. 446) — the link between data and the world — and "destabilize[s] trust in data" altogether (H&W p. 448), making downstream analytics unreliable in ways that even more data cannot recover. Avoidance reduces sample size; literacy and advocacy can be answered with policy; subversion silently rots the data.

**Q6. How does the datafication of healthcare reshape professional judgement?**
Hoeyer and Wadmann argue that pervasive digitalization "has changed the conditions for professional work as well as what counts as valid knowledge about work" (p. 447). Three mechanisms operate in concert. First, *visibility*: "being seen and recognized as doing important work of sufficient quality implies the use of standardized data that can travel across contexts" (p. 444), so clinicians cannot rest on tacit clinical judgement — they must externalize it as data or it does not count. Second, *self-referentiality*: data become a "self-referential sign" (Poster, 1990, in H&W p. 446) and authority comes to rest "on better access to data and a right of interpretation – irrespective of the referentiality of those data" (p. 446). Third, the *moral landscape* shifts: clinicians take "pride in good data" detached from clinical outcome ("the patient fares 'no better and no worse'", p. 447), and Cover My Ass becomes routine. The corrosive endpoint is summarized in Milan's terms as well: when "interpretative processes are deeply affected by the algorithmic ecosystem that mediates interpersonal interactions and worldviews" (Milan, p. 6), professional autonomy contracts.

**Q7. What does Milan mean by the three transformations (infrastructure, agency, tactics), and how do they apply to a case organization?**
Milan (p. 6) holds that datafication has reshaped resistance along three dimensions. *Infrastructure* names the new structural conditions: a "digitalised and datafied public sphere" where algorithms tailor content, intrusive surveillance such as Hong Kong's smart lampposts threatens participation, and "digital commitment does not always translate into embodied action" (Dencik & Leistert, 2015, in Milan p. 6). *Political agency* names how social actors interpret and act in this environment, now constrained by Cheney-Lippold's (2011) "algorithmic identity" and forms of biopower (Foucault, 2008). *Tactics* names the action repertoire, which "continuously expands to adapt to the current socio-technical context" (Taylor & Van Dyke, 2004, in Milan p. 7) — feminist activists who "make [their] feed work for them" (Schoettler, 2023, in Milan p. 7). For a case like Rigshospitalet, these translate as: infrastructure (the EPIC/Sundhedsplatformen environment, the regional reporting hierarchy, audit by Rigsrevisionen — Hoeyer & Wadmann pp. 443–444); agency (clinicians' ability to reframe data tasks as clinically valuable or to refuse them); tactics (data massage, supplementary databases that "prove existing systems wrong", H&W p. 448, and explicit advocacy through professional press, Lange, 2017).

**Q8. The lecture quotes Hoeyer and Wadmann: "Datafication might do most harm when people believe too much in data and forget to retain room for judgement" (p. 449; slide 23). Unpack this.**
The claim inverts the standard surveillance-capitalism worry. Milan, citing Zuboff (2019), warns that datafication enables "prediction and control"; Hoeyer and Wadmann (p. 449) concede the surveillance reading but suggest its harms are mediated by *belief*: as long as managers, regulators, and clinicians treat what is in the system as "real to the organization" (p. 446), three pathologies follow — money flows to data work rather than care; data become self-referential and trust-in-numbers (Porter, 1995, in H&W p. 436) coexists with knowledge that the numbers are fiddled; and goal orientation drifts from the patient to the audit. Milan's productive tactics offer the corrective: literacy reframes data as one input among others; counter-imagination "presents 'different ways of thinking about what algorithms are and do'" (Bucher, 2017, p. 32, in Milan p. 10). The closing methodological injunction — "we need to develop theoretically what professional judgement means in data-saturated organizations" (H&W p. 449) — is itself a counter-imagination project for the academy.

**Q9. Where does avoidance fit in the contemporary data-driven society, and what are its limits?**
Milan (p. 9) defines avoidance as a defensive tactic with three sub-registers: *escape* (boycotts, refusing intrusive products), *non-cooperation* (movement-operated servers refusing the EU Data Retention Directive; covering one's face past a CCTV camera, Madison & Klang, 2019, in Milan p. 9), and *self-sufficiency* (alternative platforms — Lovink & Rasch, 2013; Couture & Toupin, 2019). The limit is structural and growing: "essential services like welfare and identity verification" increasingly require smartphone apps (Milan, p. 12; lecture slide 8), so escape is not a sustainable position. Hoeyer and Wadmann's data describe an organizational mirror: clinicians cannot avoid the EPIC system because being "invisible" in the data is more dangerous than being misrepresented (Star, 1991, in H&W p. 441). Both papers therefore push the analytic weight onto productive resistance — literacy, counter-imagination, advocacy — and onto the *redesign* of the assemblage rather than withdrawal from it.

**Q10. How would you envision resistance and meaningless work for the Rigshospitalet or Will & Agency cases (lecture slides 20, 25)?**
At Rigshospitalet, resistance is likely to look exactly as Hoeyer and Wadmann describe it: clinicians "fiddle" and "massage" mandatory regional indicators (e.g. the time of final diagnosis, p. 442); ward managers maintain "supplementary statistics to prove the centralized monitoring system wrong" (p. 443); patient activists pursue *advocacy* through patient organizations to make patient-reported data count (Langstrup, 2019, in H&W p. 442). Meaningless work is whatever data is produced "to keep the system silent" (p. 439) — pharmaceutical screenings of relatives logged as patients; first-symptom dates that "really, [are] quite arbitrary" (p. 442). At Will & Agency the analysis runs through Milan: an agency managing creative talent and audience-data products will encounter *self-defence* (talent obscuring personal data), *subversion* (creators "polluting" engagement signals to manipulate platform algorithms — Brunton & Nissenbaum, 2013, p. 171), and *avoidance* (creators leaving the platform — Kaun & Treré, 2018, in Milan p. 9). For both case companies the recommendation pattern is the same: name the resistance tactic, locate its target in the data pipeline, and ask whether the organization has confused data-as-symbol with data-as-decision-support (Feldman & March, 1981, in H&W p. 447).

## 7. One-minute elevator answer

Week 9 is about the discontents of datafication — what people *do* when they push back. Milan gives us a six-tactic typology, split into defensive resistance (self-defence, subversion, avoidance) that targets the technological infrastructure, and productive resistance (literacy, counter-imagination, advocacy campaigning) that targets its symbols and norms. She argues the future belongs to productive resistance because escape is no longer practical: too much of life now runs through smartphone apps. Hoeyer and Wadmann take us inside a Danish hospital and show what happens when resistance stays small and silent — clinicians fiddle, massage data, take pride in good data unhooked from clinical outcome, and end up doing "meaningless work" that no one in the chain feels able to stop. The implication for our case work is dual: read complaints and workarounds as signals about where the data assemblage has come uncoupled from the organization's goal, and design data systems with productive resistance in mind, not against it.
