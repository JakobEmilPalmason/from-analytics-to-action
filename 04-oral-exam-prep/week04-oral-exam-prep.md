# Week 4 — Datafication: Oral Exam Prep

## 1. Week overview

This week opens Theme 2 ("Making Data Valuable") by treating data not as a found resource but as something *made*. Datafication is the set of processes through which slices of human life and organizational activity are abstracted into quantified, machine-readable form, and through which that quantified material is rendered valuable for some actors and not others. The central question is therefore reflexive: *why do we have the data we have, whose interests does it serve, what does it allow us to know — and could it be otherwise?* Mejias and Couldry (2019) supply the concept and its critical pedigree (political economy, legal theory, decolonial theory); Flyverbom and Madsen (2015) supply a working typology — production, structuring, distribution, visualization — for tracing the situated choices that turn raw traces into actionable knowledge. The practical pay-off is that students should be able to interrogate their own case dataset by locating the choices, interests, and frictions at each of these four "analytical moments."

## 2. Key concepts

- **Datafication** — "the quantification of human life through digital information, very often for economic value" (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 1); the verb form signals that *something is made into data*, not found.
- **Data vs. capta** — Kitchin (2014) argues "data" (Latin: *given*) misleads; what we have is *capta* — what is *captured* — emphasizing selection and abstraction (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 2).
- **"Raw data is an oxymoron"** — Bowker (2008) / Gitelman (2013): data is always already produced, classified, and framed; treating it as raw hides consequences (lecture; Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 2).
- **Datafication vs. digitization** — Cukier & Mayer-Schönberger (2013): digitization converts analog signals into bits; datafication renders phenomena into quantified, analyzable form. "Digitalization turbocharges datafication. But it is not a substitute" (lecture, slide 11).
- **Computed sociality / platformed sociality** — Alaimo & Kallinikos (2017); Van Dijck (2013): social life reorganized so its conduct can be continually fed back as data (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 3).
- **Social quantification sector** — Couldry & Mejias's term for the corporate-state ecology (Big Tech, brokers, analytics firms) whose business is extracting and processing life as data (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 2).
- **Surveillance capitalism** — Zuboff (2015, 2019): a stage of capitalism in which human experience itself becomes the raw material of behavioural-prediction products (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 2).
- **Data colonialism** — Couldry & Mejias (2019): datafication appropriates social resources for profit in a manner functionally analogous to historical colonialism — the function is "to dispossess" (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 6).
- **Nodocentrism** — Mejias (2013): networks recognize only nodes; whatever cannot be coded as a node "does not exist" for the system (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 4).
- **Sorting** — umbrella term Flyverbom & Madsen (2015, p. 144) borrow from Bowker & Star (1999) to cover classification, categorization, quantification, calculation, valuation and commensuration.
- **Four analytical moments** — Flyverbom & Madsen's (2015) typology: **Production** (translating conduct/objects into a data stream), **Structuring** (databases, metadata, classification), **Distribution** (negotiating access between owners and users), **Visualization** (turning data into actionable representations).
- **Affordances** — Gibson (1977) / Hutchby (2001) via Flyverbom & Madsen: technologies are not neutral tools; they enable and restrict particular actions, shaping the data that becomes possible (p. 146-147).
- **Big data as boundary object** — Star & Griesemer (1989) via Flyverbom & Madsen (2015, p. 158): "big data" is not one thing but a label constantly re-framed to mediate between projects.
- **Inscription** — Latour (1986): visualizations function as inscriptions that "draw things together" into circulable evidence (Flyverbom & Madsen, 2015, p. 154-155).
- **Reactivity / classification situations** — Espeland & Sauder (2007); Fourcade & Healy (2013): once classification schemes are in place, the social world reorganizes itself around them (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 4).

## 3. Per-paper notes

### Mejias, U. A. & Couldry, N. (2019), "Datafication," *Internet Policy Review* 8(4)

**(a) Core argument.** Datafication names a *contemporary* and *historically specific* process — the quantification of human life through digital infrastructures, predominantly for economic value — that cannot be separated from the infrastructures collecting the data and the value-generation processes monetizing it. The article maps how three critical traditions (political economy, legal studies, decolonial theory) read datafication's consequences, all of them converging on "the intersection of power and knowledge" (Abstract).

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Conceptual review essay. No original empirics; the paper is a literature synthesis around the concept of datafication, drawing on Kitchin, Gitelman, Van Dijck, Zuboff, Cohen, and the authors' own *Costs of Connection* (2019).

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- "Datafication is not just the making of information... datafication is a contemporary phenomenon which refers to the quantification of human life through digital information, very often for economic value" (Abstract, p. 1).
- "Data is the 'material produced by abstracting the world into categories, measures and other representational forms... that constitute the building blocks from which information and knowledge are created'" (citing Kitchin, 2014, p. 1; Mejias & Couldry, p. 1-2).
- "The production of data cannot be separated from two essential elements: the external infrastructure via which it is collected, processed and stored, and the processes of value generation" (p. 2).
- "Data colonialism... appropriates human life so that data can be continuously extracted from it for the benefit of particular (Western, but also increasingly global capitalist) interests... the function remains the same: to dispossess" (p. 6).
- On the "data is the new oil" trope: "this metaphor 'sidesteps evaluation of any misappropriation or exploitation that might arise from data use'" (citing Scholz, 2018, p. 2; Mejias & Couldry, p. 4).

**(d) Connection to the week.** Provides the *concept* and the *critical stakes*. It establishes (i) that data is made, not found; (ii) that the actors making it (the social quantification sector) shape what becomes knowable; and (iii) that this is not technologically neutral but a "matrix of actors and structures" with distributive consequences. It is the lens through which Flyverbom & Madsen's situated cases should be read.

### Flyverbom, M. & Madsen, A. (2015), "Sorting data out — unpacking big data value chains and algorithmic knowledge production"

**(a) Core argument.** "Big data" diagnoses (data economy, algorithmic culture, filter bubbles, end of politics) operate at too high a level of abstraction. To understand datafication's actual social and political consequences we need a *fine-grained, situated* vocabulary. The chapter offers that vocabulary as a "data value chain" of four analytical moments — production, structuring, distribution, visualization — and shows that "big data" is in fact a boundary object whose meaning differs project by project.

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Conceptual chapter combined with two contrasting case studies: Copenhagen Solutions Lab (CSL — sensor-based smart-city project, "Internet of Things") and UN Global Pulse (GP — social-media-monitoring lab for crisis early warning). Material is qualitative/observational and mobilizes STS, sociology of valuation, and affordance theory.

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- "We use sorting as an umbrella concept for a range of analytical processes including classification, categorization, quantification, calculation, valuation and commensuration" (p. 144).
- "Data is not found or 'raw' — it is carefully produced to fit an organizational and societal context" (p. 149).
- "It is solely the company that decides on the formats in which the data is produced and the metadata that accompanies it... none of them are decided by the GP and they can change with very short notice" (p. 152, on Twitter as a structuring authority outside GP's control).
- "Decisions about data formats and metadata are not just technical issues. These choices reveal strategies of sorting that will ultimately have consequences for the kind of visualizations that can be produced" (p. 152-153).
- "Big data is not valuable because it comes with a specific algorithmic culture... The value of big data projects differ from situation to situation... big data is a boundary object that is constantly re-framed" (p. 158).

**(d) Connection to the week.** Operationalizes the concept Mejias & Couldry give us. It supplies the *toolkit* — the four moments — for examining where datafication choices actually get made in an organization, who makes them, and what gets foreclosed. Particularly relevant for the week's case work: students can locate their own dataset on this typology and ask, at each moment, *who decided what?*

## 4. Lecture connections

Anders Koed Madsen frames the lecture (slides 7-11) by sharpening the *datafication ≠ digitization* distinction (Cukier & Mayer-Schönberger): scanning a page is digitization; OCR is datafication. He stresses that organizations frequently invest in digitization without thinking through datafication's "bi-effects" (slide 10) — the ticketing-system example illustrates that *what* gets recorded as data downstream is determined by upstream choices that often look like ordinary IT decisions.

He then historicizes the practice (slides 12-15): cadastral maps, censuses, "the world should be taxed" — datafication is older than its digital form, and contemporary practices still carry traces of pre-digital measurement logics. Bowker's "raw data is an oxymoron" is treated as the conceptual hinge of the week (slide 16), explicitly mirrored in the week description's prompt to students.

The middle of the lecture (slides 17-20) develops "datafication battles are social battles" — corporations, states, civil-society actors, and even people inside the same organization (e.g. researchers vs. management on research-quality metrics, slide 19) fight over how the world should be turned into data and indexes. Epstein on AIDS activism and Lury & Gross on the Argentine CPI are mobilized as cases.

The Flyverbom & Madsen typology is then presented (slides 27-34) as a practical instrument: the lecturer walks through CSL and GP one moment at a time, showing the *technology* row and the *context* row of each cell. The ECHOLAB / museum-AI example (slides 37-43) demonstrates a contemporary datafication choice at the level of production. The lecture closes (slide 45) with the explicit prompt for case-company presentations: *how was your data made? who made it with what interests? what would need to change at production / structuring / distribution to address the challenge? what can be done within the existing datafication, and with what reservations?*

## 5. Cross-paper synthesis

- **Agreement on "data is made, not found."** Both papers reject the rawness of data. Mejias & Couldry root this in Kitchin and Gitelman; Flyverbom & Madsen invoke Bowker explicitly: "Raw data is an oxymoron and a bad idea" (p. 151). For both, this is not a philosophical aside but the foundation of any serious analysis of datafication's effects.
- **Disagreement on register.** Mejias & Couldry are *macro-critical*: their stakes are political economy, surveillance capitalism, data colonialism, dispossession at civilizational scale. Flyverbom & Madsen are *micro-empirical*: their stakes are the situated choices inside a municipal lab and a UN unit. The two papers complement each other — but Flyverbom & Madsen would resist the "sweeping diagnoses" Mejias & Couldry's tradition can produce, while Mejias & Couldry would worry that pure situated analysis underplays structural power.
- **Both treat infrastructure as constitutive, not incidental.** Mejias & Couldry: "The production of data cannot be separated from... the external infrastructure via which it is collected, processed and stored, and the processes of value generation" (p. 2). Flyverbom & Madsen: technologies have "distinctive organizing properties" via affordances (p. 147); the design of Twitter's interface and API determines what UN Global Pulse can know. Same insight, different vocabulary.
- **Both name a politics inside organizations.** Mejias & Couldry locate it primarily *between* corporations / states / civic actors. Flyverbom & Madsen show that the same conflicts run *inside* organizations — Copenhagen's "zero-failure" subunits vs. pragmatic ones over metadata; municipal procurement and branding interests vs. the "open database" ideal. The lecture's research-quality example (slide 19) sits squarely in this register.
- **Extension: the typology as a tool for resistance.** Mejias & Couldry close on the question of how to "stand outside datafication" (p. 7). Flyverbom & Madsen do not frame their work as resistance, but their four moments give a practical vocabulary for *where* contestation can occur — production choices can be renegotiated, metadata standards challenged, distribution arrangements opened or closed. For the case-work prompt, this is the actionable bridge between the two readings.

## 6. Likely exam questions

**Q1. What does it mean to say "raw data is an oxymoron and a bad idea"? Use both readings.**
Bowker's claim, cited approvingly by both Gitelman (in Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 2) and Flyverbom & Madsen (2015, p. 151), is that the apparent rawness of data hides the abstraction, selection, and classification work that *produces* it. Mejias & Couldry develop this into a critique of metaphors like "data is the new oil," which "sidestep evaluation of any misappropriation or exploitation" (p. 4) and naturalize an extractive process. Flyverbom & Madsen show concretely what is hidden: at CSL, the "data" reflects municipal strategy and infrastructure projects; at UN Global Pulse, it reflects Twitter's interface design and API policy. The phrase is "a bad idea" because *acting* on data as if it were raw means acting blind to the interests baked into its production. The exam answer should pivot from concept to consequence: *if* data is never raw, *then* every analytic decision downstream inherits upstream politics.

**Q2. Walk through Flyverbom and Madsen's four analytical moments using your own case as the example.**
Production: where do the data points come from — sensors, transactions, user clicks, manual entry — and who decided to record *these* events and not others? Structuring: what are the database schemas, taxonomies, and metadata, and whose interests do they serve (e.g. the CSL "zero-failure" vs. pragmatic factions, p. 152)? Distribution: who can access the data, on what terms, and what frictions exist (procurement contracts, branding, legitimacy inside the org, p. 154-155)? Visualization: what representations have already been built, and which actors take them as evidence? Mejias & Couldry sharpen this by asking, at each moment, *who is the social-quantification-sector actor here* and what value is being generated (p. 2-3)? The point is not to fill in a table but to identify where in the chain the case challenge could realistically be reframed.

**Q3. How do Mejias and Couldry distinguish datafication from digitization, and why does the distinction matter?**
Datafication is the rendering of phenomena into quantified, analyzable form (Mayer-Schönberger and Cukier, 2013, in Mejias & Couldry, p. 1); digitization is the conversion of analog signal into binary code. The lecture's Google example is illustrative: scanning a book is digitization; OCR-ing it into searchable, indexable text is datafication (slide 9). The distinction matters because organizations routinely conflate the two (slide 10, the Copenhagen ticketing system) and so make strategic digitization decisions without thinking through the data byproducts. Mejias & Couldry add that datafication, unlike digitization, has a built-in drive — "the datafication of everything" (p. 2) — because each datafied domain becomes a candidate for automated analysis at scale, which is what makes the social quantification sector economically possible.

**Q4. What is data colonialism, and how does it differ from "data is the new oil"?**
Couldry and Mejias (2019) argue that datafication is "not just in the metaphorical sense" but "literally a new mode of data colonialism" that appropriates *social resources* the way historical colonialism appropriated territory and labour, and the function "remains the same: to dispossess" (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 6). The "data is the new oil" trope shares the extractive intuition but, as Scholz (2018) argues, "sidesteps evaluation of any misappropriation" (p. 4) by treating data as a natural substance to be mined. Data colonialism differs precisely because it foregrounds the *appropriation* — life is not lying around as ore; it is restructured ("computed sociality," Alaimo and Kallinikos, 2017) so that data can be continuously extracted from it. The decolonial reading also names the geography of the extraction: the social quantification sector is overwhelmingly headquartered in the US and China.

**Q5. Flyverbom and Madsen describe big data as a "boundary object." What do they mean and what is the analytical pay-off?**
Borrowing from Star & Griesemer (1989), they argue that "big data" is "a boundary object that is constantly re-framed to fit specific situations and used to mediate between different projects and aspirations" (p. 158). The CSL smart-city project and the UN Global Pulse social-media monitoring project both fall under "big data," but their production technologies (sensors vs. APIs), structuring authorities (municipality vs. Twitter), distribution logics (open portal vs. internal legitimation), and visualizations (geo-maps vs. anomaly indicators) are so different that grouping them under a single "data paradigm" obscures more than it reveals (p. 156-157). The analytical pay-off is that we should resist sweeping diagnoses (Anderson's "end of theory," Pariser's filter bubbles, Morozov's death of politics) and instead ask, project by project, *what kind of big data are we dealing with?* This pairs naturally with Mejias & Couldry's claim that the matrix of actors and structures is where the interesting analysis lives.

**Q6. Where do Mejias and Couldry and Flyverbom and Madsen disagree, and which view do you find more useful for case work?**
They share the foundational claim that data is made, not found. They diverge on register: Mejias & Couldry are macro-critical, naming systemic forces (surveillance capitalism, data colonialism, the social quantification sector); Flyverbom & Madsen are micro-empirical, tracing the situated choices inside two organizational sites. Mejias & Couldry might say the four-moments typology, used alone, risks normalizing datafication as a managerial design problem and missing the structural extraction it serves. Flyverbom & Madsen might say macro diagnoses are too distant from the practical work to inform intervention. For case work the typology is more directly *useful* — students need a vocabulary for what to interrogate in their own dataset — but it should be *anchored* by the Mejias & Couldry frame: the four moments are *where* power gets exercised, but the political-economic stakes (whose value, whose dispossession) are *why* it matters.

**Q7. Why do Flyverbom and Madsen consider the Production moment so consequential, and what makes it different in CSL vs. UN Global Pulse?**
The production moment determines what is even visible to subsequent analysis. At CSL, production is shaped from inside the organization: data points are selected to fit municipal strategies (the "Copenhagen-stories," CO2-neutrality by 2025) and existing infrastructure projects, so what becomes a smart city is "a city of moveable entities on a geo-coordinated map" (p. 149). At GP, production is *outsourced* to platform companies whose interface design (the 140-character tweet), terms of service, and API policies decide what crisis-related stress *looks like* (p. 150). The contrast matters: CSL retains political control of production but inherits the strategic priors built into "Copenhagen-stories"; GP enjoys a wider data stream but cannot guarantee its stability or its representativeness. Mejias & Couldry's point that "the production of data cannot be separated from the external infrastructure" (p. 2) is concretely illustrated here.

**Q8. The week's prompt asks "could it be otherwise?" — how would you answer for a case dataset?**
The answer requires walking the four moments and naming, at each, an alternative that would have made the analytic question more tractable. At Production: were the right entities datafied, or are events the company cares about (e.g., near-misses, unsolicited feedback, qualitative observations) absent because no one chose to record them? At Structuring: do the schemas reflect operational categories at the cost of analytic ones, and what would it cost to add metadata? At Distribution: are there silos inside the organization whose data the project never sees? At Visualization: which representations already circulate and frame what counts as a problem? Mejias & Couldry remind us that "could it be otherwise" is also a political question — the existing datafication serves particular interests, and changes have winners and losers. Flyverbom & Madsen show that some changes are realistic (negotiated metadata, opening procurement contracts to data-sharing) and some are not (UN cannot make Twitter rebuild its API).

**Q9. Mejias and Couldry list political economy, legal theory, and decolonial theory as critical lenses. Briefly distinguish them.**
Political economy reads datafication as a stage of capitalism: Marxist accounts focus on user "labour" (Fuchs and Mosco, 2017) and Zuboff (2015, 2019) names "surveillance capitalism," in which behavioural data becomes the raw material of a new commodity. Couldry and Mejias's own twist is that the fundamental move is not labour but the *abstracting force of the commodity* itself (p. 5). Legal theory frames datafication as a threat to autonomy and to "the minimal integrity of the self as a self" (p. 6), driving the GDPR's framing of personal-data protection as a fundamental right (Recital 2). Decolonial theory situates datafication in the longer history of dispossession, treating it as a "land grab" for social resources between US and Chinese social-quantification sectors (p. 7). Each lens picks out a different consequence; together they refuse the framing of datafication as a neutral technical development.

**Q10. What is "nodocentrism" and why does it matter for analytics work?**
Mejias (2013) coins nodocentrism to capture the logic of network-based datafication: "in a network, nodes only recognise other nodes, and if something is not represented as a node it does not exist" (Mejias & Couldry, 2019, p. 4). For analytics work this is the limiting case of the production-moment problem: what cannot be coded as a relation between nodes is invisible to the analysis, and what cannot be measured is paranodal (p. 8). In a case dataset this manifests as silent absences — categories of customer, kind of behaviour, or contextual relation that the schema cannot represent. The implication is methodological humility: any conclusion drawn from network or graph data is conditional on what the network was built to recognize, and recommendations to the case company should name what the data *cannot* see, not just what it shows.

## 7. One-minute elevator answer

Week 4 is about datafication: the processes that turn slices of life into machine-readable data, and the choices, interests, and power relations baked into those processes. Mejias and Couldry give us the concept and its stakes — datafication is contemporary, infrastructural, and tied to economic value, and critical traditions read it as surveillance capitalism, a threat to autonomy, or a new form of colonial dispossession. Flyverbom and Madsen give us a working tool: four analytical moments — production, structuring, distribution, visualization — at each of which sorting and valuation choices get made. Together the readings rule out treating data as raw or natural and replace that with a question: *who made this data, with what interests, and what does it foreclose?* Practically, this is the lens for our case work. Before any modelling, locate the dataset on the four moments and ask whether what you cannot see matters more than what you can.
