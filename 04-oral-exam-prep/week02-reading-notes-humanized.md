## 1. Zammuto et al. (2007) — Why information technology changed how organisations work

**Elevator summary.** For most of the last century, organisation theorists assumed that the org chart was the thing holding organisations together. IT was background plumbing: it sped things up but didn't change how work was organised. Zammuto and his co-authors say that view is out of date. By the 1990s, IT had stopped being plumbing and started replacing the org chart itself. Integrated systems, dashboards, ERP, and the web began doing coordination work that hierarchy used to do. Their answer: rethink "organisation" as a fabric in which IT is now one of the threads, and analyse it through "affordances," meaning what new ways of working become possible when particular technologies meet particular organisational features.

### The big argument

The paper opens with a puzzle. Between 1996 and 2005, only about 1.2% of articles in the top organisation studies journals seriously dealt with IT. Over the same decade, internet hosts grew from 9 million to nearly 400 million. Organisation theory had been quietly looking the other way while its own subject matter was being rewired. Zammuto's worry is straightforward: if the things we study change because of IT, our theories go stale unless they catch up.

So why did organisation theorists stop caring about technology? In the 1960s and 1970s, contingency theory had pushed technology to the centre of the field. The idea was that the right organisational structure depends on factors like technology, size, and environment. Then contingency theory got displaced by approaches that focused on the environment instead, like institutional theory, population ecology, and resource dependence. At the same time, IT in the 1980s really was mostly automation. It accelerated existing patterns of work without changing them. So scholars could justify ignoring it. Zammuto calls this old role "automated plumbing."

The shift came in the 1990s. Enterprise systems (think ERP: software that lets every part of the firm read from the same database) integrated information across functions. Suddenly the "bureau" in "bureaucracy" could be opened up. Zammuto literally invokes the image of a chest of drawers, with marketing, accounting, manufacturing each in its own drawer. Anyone in the process could see the whole flow. That changed the nature of work. Instead of moving information up and down through managers, people could organise around the work itself. Hierarchy started losing its monopoly on coordination, and IT started taking some of it over. That's what the authors mean when they say IT has become "one of the threads from which the fabric of organization is now woven."

To talk about this without falling into the old traps (technological determinism, where technology causes everything, or social constructivism, where people just decide what technology means), they import the concept of *affordances* from Gibson and Hutchby. An affordance is what a particular combination of technology and organisational features makes possible. A door handle, in their nice analogy, doesn't mean anything by itself. It only makes sense alongside the people who open doors. In the same way, an ERP system has different consequences in a leading-edge manufacturer than in a resource-poor company. Same software, different organisational context, different affordances. The point is that you can't understand IT without understanding the organisation, or the other way around. The action is at the intersection.

The authors then offer five concrete affordances. This is the part of the paper that always shows up in exams. Visualising entire work processes. Real-time / flexible product and service innovation. Virtual collaboration. Mass collaboration. Simulation and synthetic representation. Each one needs both halves: technical features (dashboards, service-oriented architectures, wikis, business intelligence) and organisational features (process standardisation, common ground, psychological safety, norms of reciprocity, willingness to act on what a simulation shows).

### Key concepts (with everyday-language definitions)

#### Affordance

An affordance is what a thing makes possible *in combination with* the people and the setting around it. (Plain English: what you can actually do with X, given who you are and where you are.) The classic example is a door handle. The handle "affords" pulling, but only if there's a person there with hands and a reason to walk through. Zammuto extends this to organisations: an ERP system affords cross-functional visibility, but only if there are people, process standards, and a reward system that make using that visibility worthwhile.

*Why this matters for the exam:* affordance is the single most important concept of the paper, probably of the whole week. It's the lens that lets you avoid the false binary of "technology causes change" vs "people cause change." If the examiner asks anything about how technology and organisation relate, the answer almost always involves affordances.

#### Organisational fabric

The "fabric of organisation" is a metaphor: an organisation is held together by intertwined threads. Pull one, the whole thing changes shape. The original main thread was hierarchy: the boss tells you, you tell your reports, information goes up, instructions come down. Zammuto's argument is that IT is now also one of those threads. You can't separate the social weave from the digital weave. They are spun together.

*Why this matters for the exam:* this metaphor gives the paper its title. It also lets me say something memorable: "Hierarchy used to be the only thread. Now IT is woven into the fabric too."

#### Forms of organising (versus organisational form)

Old organisation theory asked, "What form does this organisation take?" — functional, divisional, matrix. Zammuto says we should switch to asking, "What forms of *organising* are emerging here?" Don't think of organisations as fixed buildings. Think of them as ongoing patterns of activity that can shift. The reason: with IT-enabled visibility, work no longer has to be divided into rigid vertical slices. It can flow around the actual problem.

*Why this matters for the exam:* it shows I understand the deeper move the paper is making. He isn't just adding "IT" to old theory; he's changing the verb from "organisation" (a noun) to "organising" (a verb).

#### Sociotechnical perspective

The older idea, going back to Trist in the 1950s, that the social and technical sides of work always come together and have to be designed together. You can't design a good factory by perfecting the machines and ignoring the people, or the other way around. Zammuto says the field needs to *rediscover* this view because IT is now so deeply intertwined with social relations.

*Why this matters for the exam:* if asked where the affordance idea comes from, I can say it builds on a long sociotechnical tradition that the field had forgotten.

#### Productivity paradox (and its resolution)

In the 1980s, researchers found that companies investing more in IT didn't always become more productive. They called this the "productivity paradox." Then in the 1990s the picture changed: IT investment began to pay off, but only in firms that *also* invested in organisational change, like decentralised decision-making, training, and business process redesign. Buying more software doesn't help unless you also change how you work.

*Why this matters for the exam:* this is the empirical anchor of the whole paper. It's the smoking-gun evidence that IT and organisation have to be studied together, because alone neither explains performance.

### The example(s) the author uses

I'll walk through the five affordances by their illustrations, because the examples are what make the concepts concrete.

*Visualising entire work processes.* Wal-Mart's logistics tracking system lets every employee and supplier watch a product move from warehouse to shelf to shopping cart in real time. Technical side: dashboards, integrated databases, RFID chips. Organisational side: process standardisation, KPIs, decentralised structures, and a reward system that makes people accountable for how their work fits the whole. With both in place, people stop guarding their drawer and start helping the whole flow.

*Real-time / flexible product and service creation.* A company can plug Google Maps into its store-finder page in an afternoon. ETrade can build a new account-opening flow in days by stitching together pre-built software components. Technical features: service-oriented architectures, open-source software, standardised interfaces. Organisational features: common ground (shared concepts so people from different specialities can talk), boundary spanners (people who carry ideas across teams), and a culture that lets groups remix things without asking permission.

*Virtual collaboration.* Distributed teams sharing knowledge through chat, documents, and shared workspaces. Technical features: subject-line tagging, document linking, asynchronous tools. Organisational features: psychological safety (people feel safe enough to share), situational awareness (people know what others are doing), and sometimes occasional face-to-face meetings to anchor the rest. Without those, distributed teams fragment, hoard knowledge, and split into sub-groups.

*Mass collaboration.* Wikipedia. Prediction markets. The KatrinaWiki, where a student in Amsterdam coordinated thousands of strangers to post missing-person notices and emergency response info after Hurricane Katrina. Technical features: reversion histories, usage statistics, secure logons. Organisational features: community-owned moderation, norms of reciprocity, and ways for contributors to gain recognition. The interesting part is that organisations can be temporarily *constituted* by strangers around a task, then dissolve.

*Simulation / synthetic representation.* "What-if" tools. Dashboards that flip into hypothetical mode. Business intelligence that projects forward. Toyota using Second Life to test car configurations. The catch: simulation only works if the organisation is willing to *act* on what it shows. Zammuto cites a case where a supervisor used a simulation tool to find a process improvement, but management refused to implement her proposal because it required too many changes. The tool was never used again. Technology plus unwillingness equals waste.

### How this applies to my case (Publikum / arthouse / IMDb data)

The arthouse cohort definition I'm working with is itself an affordance in Zammuto's sense. A hand-built rule alone, or an LLM score alone, would not produce the same possibilities. The hybrid (rule OR LLM ≥ 8) is what you get when you combine a technical feature (a large language model that can read plot summaries) with an organisational practice (a hand-curated rule reflecting the team's collective judgment about what counts as arthouse). The cohort is what it is *because* the team chose this particular combination. A different team, with different priors, would have produced a different cohort. That's exactly Zammuto's point: features alone don't explain outcomes; combinations do.

The "visualising entire work processes" affordance maps directly onto what the analysis notebook tries to do. The 14-section EDA notebook with its co-production network, genre co-occurrence map, and four-quadrant ratings plot is an attempt to give Publikum a dashboard view of 2,000 European films at once. The IMDb equivalent of Wal-Mart's logistics tracking. But Zammuto would also remind me that *the visualisation only matters if the organisational features are there too.* If Publikum doesn't have a process for acting on what the visualisation reveals, the affordance doesn't materialise. The dashboard becomes pretty pictures.

The decision questions Publikum cares about (positioning, target audiences, market and country strategy, comparable titles) are *organisational* questions, not technical ones. Zammuto's point is that data alone can't answer them. They require the data plus the analytics capability plus the organisational habits for using insight. Concrete example: if the comparable-title analysis suggests an upcoming arthouse project resembles past Belgian-French co-productions that under-performed in Germany, that finding is only useful if Publikum has the habit of reading and incorporating it into pitch decks. So my job isn't just producing the model. It's producing it in a form that fits the affordance: clear enough to act on, embedded in a process the team uses.

The "real-time / flexible product and service creation" affordance also resonates. The enrichment pipeline (TMDb + IMDb GraphQL + MovieLens) is a concrete example of stitching small components together to build a new analytical service. That's service-oriented architecture in miniature. Each script can be rerun, each data source can be swapped, the merge key (`titleId`) is the standard interface. This is exactly the modular logic Zammuto describes for ETrade.

### A critical view

The big limitation of Zammuto et al. is that the paper is from 2007 and is genuinely optimistic about IT. The five affordances are presented as a menu of possibilities, with a brief acknowledgement that they "may not manifest" without the right intent. The dark side of those same affordances barely gets a paragraph. Mass collaboration on social platforms is presented through the heroic case of the KatrinaWiki, not through troll farms or coordinated harassment. Simulation is presented as decision support, not as the kind of model that gives an illusion of control. In hindsight, this paper is the prelude to the surveillance, platform-power and invisible-labour critiques that the rest of the week's readings raise. Zammuto opens the black box of "IT and organisation," but the box he opens is the cleanly engineered one. Justesen & Plesner's invisible digi-workers, Flyverbom & Murray's algorithmically recognisable manipulation, even Galbraith's power shifts in favour of analytics experts, all complicate Zammuto's largely positive story. A strong answer should acknowledge this. Zammuto gives us the lens (affordance, fabric, forms of organising), but the picture seen through that lens has gotten more complicated since.

### Two or three quotes worth knowing

> "IT has supplanted many of the coordination and control roles of hierarchy, creating the opportunities for new forms of organizing that focus on process instead of function." (Conclusion, p. 758)

> "Hierarchy was the original thread from which the fabric of organization was woven." (p. 750)

> "One cannot talk about a complex technology without reference to the social setting, just as it makes limited sense to talk about a door handle without discussing the people opening the open doors." (p. 753)

### What an examiner is most likely to ask

**Q: Define "affordance" in this paper. Why do the authors use this term instead of just saying "what technology does"?**
An affordance is what a particular combination of a technology and an organisational setting makes possible. The reason the authors don't just say "what technology does" is that they want to avoid two traps. The first is technological determinism: the idea that technology by itself causes organisational change. The second is pure constructivism: that technology is just a blank slate onto which people project meaning. Affordance lets you say both things at once. Technology has materiality that favours some uses and constrains others, but those uses are only realised when the organisational features are right. So the same ERP can produce one set of outcomes in a leading-edge firm and a totally different set in a resource-poor one.

**Q: The authors say technology had "virtually died out as a theme" in organisation studies by the mid-1990s. Why?**
Two reasons. First, contingency theory had been displaced by approaches that focused on the environment instead — institutional theory, population ecology, resource dependence. The "environment" leg of contingency theory won, and technology lost. Second, IT in the 1980s really was mostly automation: paper to electrons, but no fundamental change in how work was organised. So scholars could plausibly ignore it. The authors point out that the world changed in the 1990s with enterprise systems and integrated databases, but the field's attention didn't catch up. That's the gap their paper is trying to close.

**Q: Walk me through the "fabric of organisation" metaphor.**
The fabric metaphor is the spine of the paper. Imagine an organisation as a piece of woven cloth. The threads hold it together. For most of the twentieth century, the main thread was hierarchy: managerial reporting lines that channelled information up and instructions down. Zammuto's argument is that IT is now also one of the threads. Enterprise systems and integrated databases coordinate work that hierarchy used to coordinate. Pull one thread, the whole organisation changes shape. The implication: you can't study or design organisations today without taking IT seriously, and you can't study IT in organisations without taking the social weave seriously.

**Q: Pick one of the five affordances and explain how the technical and organisational sides have to come together.**
Take "visualising entire work processes." On the technical side you need dashboards that show real-time status, integrated databases (typically through an ERP), and tracking sensors like RFID. But none of that gives you anything on its own. On the organisational side you need process standardisation so that the dashboard categories mean the same thing across the firm, decentralised structures so that people can act on what they see without escalating, and a reward system that holds people accountable for how their bit of work fits the whole. Without those organisational features, the dashboard just shows you problems you have no authority to fix. The affordance only emerges when both halves are there.

**Q: What's a limitation of this paper?**
It's almost twenty years old and it's optimistic. The five affordances are framed as opportunities, with a brief nod to the role of intent. But the same affordances can be used badly. Mass collaboration becomes coordinated trolling. Simulation produces false confidence. Virtual collaboration becomes surveillance. Justesen and Plesner's invisible digi-work is essentially the back-of-house cost of the affordances Zammuto celebrates. So I'd say Zammuto gives us a powerful lens, but the picture it shows has darkened in twenty years. We now need to read this paper alongside critiques that take the costs seriously.

## 2. Justesen & Plesner (2024) — The invisible work that holds digital organisations together

**Elevator summary.** When we talk about "digitalised organisations," we usually picture seamless data flows, automated systems, constant connectivity. Justesen and Plesner say this picture is misleading because it hides all the *work* that keeps that infrastructure running. They call the hidden labour "invisible digi-work" and break it into three types: *connecting* (keeping links from breaking), *compensating* (covering for what the technology can't actually do), and *cleaning* (removing junk and toxic content). This work is performed by people far from the corporate centre — Kenyan content moderators, Mechanical Turk workers, IT support staff — but also by professionals and managers in the centre who quietly absorb extra unpaid digital chores. The paper's mission is to give organisation theorists vocabulary to *see* this work, because right now the dominant metaphors hide it.

### The big argument

Justesen and Plesner are picking a fight with a particular way of writing about digital organisations. The dominant metaphors (connectivity, automation, "all-illuminated" digital spaces) present digitalised organisations as bright, frictionless, magical. Cyberspace. The cloud. Seamless data flows. The authors' first move is to call this what it is: a *root metaphor* assumption. (A root metaphor is a deep, often unconscious image that shapes how we think about a topic. "The brain is a computer" is a root metaphor. Once you accept it, certain questions feel natural — what's the processing speed? — and others feel weird — does the brain love anything?) The root metaphor of digitalisation is positive space: light, openness, smoothness, magic. And like any root metaphor, it makes some things visible and others invisible.

What it makes invisible is human labour. The authors pull on a different tradition, feminist science and technology studies, that has long argued for paying attention to the periphery, the marginal, the housekeeping that nobody pays for. From this tradition they take the classic concept of *invisible work*, which originally referred to housework, care work, emotional labour. The work that keeps everything else going but doesn't show up in formal accounts of what an organisation does.

The big claim is that today's digitalised organisations are *constituted* by invisible labour. Without people doing connecting, compensating and cleaning, the smooth digital surface doesn't exist. This is the corollary to Orlikowski and Scott's (2016) argument that "work today always entails the digital." Justesen and Plesner add: the digital always entails work, and that work is hidden by design.

The three types of invisible digi-work are the structural backbone of the paper. *Connecting* is the labour of forging, maintaining and repairing the digital links that the rhetoric of "constant connectivity" treats as automatic. *Compensating* is the labour of doing what the technology promises but can't deliver: answering the questions a chatbot fakes answering, manually verifying what the algorithm "automatically" verifies, fixing the gaps in the data the system pretends is clean. *Cleaning* is the labour of removing the disturbing, the broken, the toxic: content moderation, dataset curation, the daily clearing-out of junk email.

Each of these three types appears in three positions relative to the organisational core. Far from the core: digital microworkers in the platform economy, often outsourced and globally dispersed. In the core but far from core tasks: professionals and managers who absorb new digital chores into their working lives. Supporting the core: administrative and technical staff, especially data workers. The paper builds out a 3-by-3 matrix of types and positions. The further from the core, the more layers of devaluation pile up: economic, social, moral. The closer to the core, the more the work is devalued by the workers themselves, who don't see their own digi-housekeeping as "real work."

The whole paper is shaped by feminist STS concepts: *situatedness* (knowledge is always from somewhere, never from a "view from nowhere") and *boundary drawing* (a centre is always defined by what it excludes). The authors propose Crawford's *atlas* metaphor as an alternative to "the cloud." Instead of an abstract overhead view, an atlas zooms in on specific places, traces routes, marks boundaries, and admits that any map is partial.

### Key concepts (with everyday-language definitions)

#### Invisible digi-work

Invisible digi-work is the labour that keeps a digitalised organisation running but is rendered out of sight and undervalued. The unpaid, unnoticed or underpaid stuff people have to do for the smooth digital experience to seem smooth. The "digi" prefix matters because the authors are deliberately echoing Orlikowski and Scott's "digital work." They want their concept to sit alongside it as a *corollary*. "Digital work" describes the visible, transformed, professionalised labour of digital tools. "Invisible digi-work" describes the labour that makes that transformed work possible at all.

*Why this matters for the exam:* it's the core concept of the paper. Whatever the question, the answer runs through this concept.

#### Connecting work

The work of making, maintaining and repairing the digital connections that "constant connectivity" treats as given. Someone has to keep the wires plugged in, the credentials updated, the platforms verifying users — and that someone is usually invisible. The authors' example: when you call an Uber in Chicago, somewhere a microworker (Gray and Suri call her Ayesha) is verifying the driver's identity. You don't see her. The Uber app doesn't credit her. But without her, the connection between you and the driver doesn't happen.

*Why this matters for the exam:* connecting work is the most direct rebuttal of "frictionless platform" narratives. Bring it up if anyone says digital systems run by themselves.

#### Compensating work

Compensating work is the labour of making up for what the technology promises but can't actually deliver. Cleaning up the mess the supposedly intelligent system makes, or doing the part that was supposed to be automated. The flagship example is "fauxtomation": services advertised as AI that turn out to be powered by underpaid humans behind the scenes. The personal scheduling assistant "Amy" turned out to be teams of contract workers writing the responses by hand. But compensating work also happens at the centre. Case workers in digital public services teach citizens how to use self-service portals that were supposed to remove the case worker.

*Why this matters for the exam:* compensating work is the conceptual home of the "automation illusion." If the examiner asks about AI hype, this is the answer.

#### Cleaning work

Cleaning is the labour of removing what is perceived as dirt, mess or waste from the digital space. Cleaning datasets. Deleting toxic content. Sorting through junk mail to get to the real work. The most disturbing case: data labellers in Kenya were paid less than $2/hour to read deeply offensive material so ChatGPT could be trained to refuse it. But cleaning also happens in the core when self-employed professionals spend their mornings deleting junk email. Whiting and Symon call this *digi-housekeeping*.

*Why this matters for the exam:* cleaning has a moral charge. It's "dirty work" both literally and metaphorically. It carries the strongest case that invisibility has emotional and ethical costs, not just economic ones.

#### Mechanisms of invisibility (Hatton, drawn on by the authors)

The authors lean on Hatton's three mechanisms: *sociospatial* (the work happens somewhere else, off-site, far away), *sociolegal* (the work isn't in any contract or org chart so it doesn't legally exist), and *sociocultural* (the work is seen as something other than work, like housekeeping or emotional labour). Work can be hidden by being moved away, by not being written down, or by being culturally classified as not-work.

*Why this matters for the exam:* this is the analytical structure that lets me say *how* work becomes invisible, not just *that* it does.

### The example(s) the author uses

I'll walk through the most vivid examples in order.

*Ayesha and the Uber ride.* You stand on a curb in Chicago, open Uber, the driver arrives. Somewhere on the other side of the world, a microworker named Ayesha is verifying the driver's identity. She's invisible to you. She's invisible to the driver. She's sometimes invisible to Uber itself, because she's sub-contracted through a labour platform. She bears her own connectivity costs: if her internet drops, she loses pay. This single example contains all three types of invisibility (sociospatial, sociolegal, sociocultural) and all three forms of devaluation (economic, social, moral).

*Mechanical Turk and "Amy."* Amazon Mechanical Turk runs millions of microtasks: tagging photos, classifying texts, judging the quality of machine output. These workers compensate for what AI claims to do but can't quite do yet. The paper highlights "Amy," a supposed AI scheduling assistant that turned out to be a team of contract workers reading and rewriting messages by hand, in long shifts. The illusion of automation is held together by hidden human labour. The authors borrow Astra Taylor's term: *fauxtomation*.

*The hospital pharmacy robot.* Barrett and colleagues studied a hospital pharmacy where a dispensing robot was introduced to reduce errors. The pharmacists got more interesting professional work, more time for clinical research. But the robot generated *new* errors. Those errors were absorbed by lower-status assistants who had to feed the robot and recalibrate it, often after-hours, in a small back room. The pharmacists got the front-of-house and the prestige. The assistants got the cluttered back-end and the off-hours shifts. Same robot, different consequences depending on where you sat in the hierarchy.

*Self-employed digi-housekeepers.* Whiting and Symon's study of self-employed entrepreneurs found that they spent enormous amounts of time charging devices, syncing apps, troubleshooting connections, deleting junk email. They didn't count it as "real work," but it was the price of being able to work flexibly. Justesen and Plesner highlight this as a case of self-devaluation. The workers themselves render their digi-housekeeping invisible by not naming it as work.

*Clinical documentation integrity specialists.* Pine and Bossen describe an emerging healthcare role: people whose job is to do "translation work" between professional groups by curating data. Their work makes data sharing possible across boundaries. But their value is constantly belittled because clinicians "should be left to do their core work." They are the connective tissue, but they are described as doing not-the-real-thing.

*Kenyan data labellers and ChatGPT.* The 2023 Time exposé. Workers in Kenya were paid less than $2/hour to read deeply distressing content (sexual violence, self-harm) so the GPT model could be trained to refuse such prompts. The "clean" output of the chatbot is built on the psychic costs of cleaning workers far from any centre.

### How this applies to my case (Publikum / arthouse / IMDb data)

Justesen and Plesner's framework lights up the work I'm doing on Publikum. The arthouse cohort definition and the comparable-title analysis sit on top of an enormous amount of *invisible digi-work* that I've already done and will keep doing.

*Connecting work.* The enrichment pipeline merging IMDb, TMDb and MovieLens via `titleId` is connecting work in a very literal sense. The merge key works smoothly only because someone (me) has reconciled identifiers, handled rate limits, and designed a resume system. If I disappeared from the project, that connecting work would surface immediately as broken pipelines. Right now, in the README, the pipeline reads as "run this command, wait three hours." The work that makes that command actually run is invisible to whoever runs it next.

*Compensating work.* The hybrid arthouse rule is a compensating mechanism. The hand-built rule was never going to catch every arthouse film. It's too brittle. The LLM score isn't trustworthy on its own. It hallucinates and is biased toward English-language plot summaries. The OR combination is a compensation for the limits of each method. Every time I tweak the rule or the LLM prompt, I'm doing compensating work that hides the underlying messiness of what "arthouse" actually means. From the consumer of the cohort definition, this looks clean: 2,000 films sorted into in/out. From inside, it's a stack of judgment calls.

*Cleaning work.* The data cleaning section of the EDA notebook (parsing comma-separated fields, deriving columns, handling missing values) is cleaning work in the most literal sense. The README's column descriptions look orderly. The reality is that `allCountries` is a comma-separated string that has to be split, that `topFiveActors` is structured text mixed with IMDb IDs, that some plot fields are missing. The visualisations in `reports/figures/` are clean *because* of cleaning work that won't appear in any presentation slide.

*The four decision questions.* For each of Publikum's questions (positioning, audiences, markets, comparables), the answer the company will see is a finished recommendation. What they won't see is the connecting, compensating and cleaning that produced the answer. Justesen and Plesner give me explicit permission, even an obligation, to *make this visible* in my final deliverables. Include a "what the data does not capture" section. Document assumptions. Be transparent about the LLM's limits. The README already gestures at this; "Acknowledging biases and limitations" is in the frameworks table. The Justesen & Plesner reading is the theoretical justification for taking that gesture seriously. If the final pitch hides the digi-work, it's complicit in the same root metaphor the paper criticises.

*The team is small.* In a team of six students, the cleaning, connecting and compensating work falls disproportionately on whoever volunteers for it. That mirrors the paper's point about devaluation by self. If I think of my dataset-stitching as "not real work" and dismiss the hours spent on parsing IMDb fields, I'm reproducing the dynamic the paper warns about.

### A critical view

The paper is brilliant at naming and categorising invisible digi-work, but it's light on what to *do* about it once it has been named. The three mechanisms of invisibility (sociospatial, sociolegal, sociocultural) are diagnostic, not prescriptive. The paper ends with a call for more empirical studies and methodological innovation. Useful for academic agendas, but limited for managers who already agree their data workers are undervalued and want to know how to fix it. There's also a tension the paper doesn't fully resolve: making invisible work visible is presented as morally good, but the authors themselves note in a footnote on professional autonomy that *some* invisibility is the precondition for discretion and learning. So the politics of visibility are double-edged in a way the main argument elides. A strong answer should mention this footnote and the tension it exposes. Finally, the paper draws heavily on platform-economy cases (Uber, Mechanical Turk), where digi-work is most extreme and most visible-as-a-target-for-research. It's harder to see how the framework applies in, say, a small startup where everyone does everything and no one is structurally invisible to anyone else.

### Two or three quotes worth knowing

> "Invisible digi-work… consists of connecting, compensating and cleaning work." (p. 19, summary table)

> "Real workers remain out of sight in the service of an illusion that AI systems are autonomous and magically intelligent." (Crawford 2021, quoted on p. 13)

> "Practices of data work are often invisible to managers, and data-work tasks get neither the necessary resources nor the proper compensation." (Møller et al. 2020, quoted on p. 14)

> "Even though automation may save labour, it may also result in additional labour." (Scroggins & Pasquetto 2020, quoted on p. 16)

### What an examiner is most likely to ask

**Q: Define invisible digi-work and explain why the authors think the concept is needed.**
Invisible digi-work is the labour that establishes, maintains and repairs the digitalised organisation but is rendered out of sight and undervalued. The authors argue the concept is needed because the dominant metaphors of digitalisation (cloud, connectivity, frictionless automation) make this labour invisible by design. If we only study the visible, transformed work that digital tools enable, we miss what makes that transformation possible. So invisible digi-work is offered as a *corollary* to "digital work." Where digital work describes the visible side, invisible digi-work describes the hidden side that constitutes it.

**Q: What are the three types of invisible digi-work?**
Connecting, compensating, cleaning. Connecting is the labour of forging, maintaining and repairing digital links, like the microworker who verifies an Uber driver's identity behind the scenes. Compensating is the labour of doing what the technology promises but can't actually deliver, like the contract workers behind the supposedly AI assistant "Amy." Cleaning is the labour of removing what is perceived as dirt, mess or toxicity, like the Kenyan workers who labelled offensive content to make ChatGPT safer, or like deleting junk email at the start of your day.

**Q: Where does this invisible work happen?**
Three positions. Far from the core: globally dispersed microworkers, often outsourced, often economically precarious, working under sociospatial and sociolegal mechanisms that make them invisible. In the core but far from core tasks: professionals, self-employed entrepreneurs and managers who absorb new digital chores and devalue them themselves as "not real work." Supporting the core: data workers, technicians, administrative staff who are physically close but socially undervalued by professionals around them. The further from the core, the more layers of devaluation pile up.

**Q: How does this paper challenge Zammuto?**
Zammuto presents affordances as opportunities created when IT and organisational features are well combined. Justesen and Plesner add: *yes, but who pays the price?* The frictionless platform is frictionless because someone is absorbing the friction. The "magic" of automation is magic because the human labour is hidden. So while Zammuto opens the black box of "IT and organisation," Justesen and Plesner open a second black box that Zammuto leaves shut: the back-of-house labour. Both papers can be read together. They describe the same phenomenon from different sides. Zammuto looks at the new forms of organising the affordances make possible. Justesen and Plesner look at the invisible work the affordances depend on.

**Q: Why feminist STS specifically?**
Because feminist STS has the longest tradition of paying attention to peripheries, margins, and undervalued work. The original *invisible work* concept came from feminist research on housework and care work: labour that keeps everything else going but doesn't show up on the wage ledger. The authors borrow this lens for digital organisations because the same dynamic plays out. Digital infrastructures depend on labour that doesn't make it into the org chart. So the feminist STS framing isn't decoration. It gives them the conceptual machinery (situatedness, boundary drawing, ecologies of visibility) that lets them see what the dominant root metaphors hide.

## 3. Flyverbom & Murray (2018) — Datastructuring: how digital traces get organised before we ever see them

**Elevator summary.** When we read a tweet, watch a YouTube video, or get recommended a Netflix show, we usually focus on the *content*: what the tweet says, what the video shows. Flyverbom and Murray say there's a more important layer underneath. The way data is *structured* before it ever reaches us. Algorithms, metadata, friend networks, search rankings, hashtags, content moderation rules: all of these quietly organise what is even visible to us. The authors call this layer "datastructuring," and they argue it has become a kind of hidden grammar of contemporary life. The forms (the way data is sorted) increasingly matter more than the content. That's what allows political manipulation, commercial targeting, and even the meaning of words to shift without most of us noticing.

### The big argument

The paper opens with a bold claim about communication theory. Scholars usually study the *content* of messages or the *distribution* of messages, but they neglect the *conditions* of communication: the technological and infrastructural arrangements that determine which messages can be sent and seen at all. As digital technologies and processes of *datafication* (turning human activity into data) spread, those conditions are no longer neutral plumbing. They are productive forces. Flyverbom and Murray want us to take the conditions seriously.

To do that, they propose the umbrella concept of *datastructuring*: the practices through which data gets organised in digital spaces, and the way that organisation in turn shapes social action. The concept is meant to bridge three existing literatures that don't usually talk to each other: infrastructure studies (the physical layer of cables, servers, protocols), platform studies (the rules and architectures of social media and similar services), and algorithm studies (the software logic that sorts, ranks, recommends). Each of these tells a piece of the story. Flyverbom and Murray want a concept that captures the whole.

They identify four features of datastructures. *Socio-material entanglement*: never just technical or just human, always both. *Recursive informating*: they fold new data back into themselves, learning and growing. *Flexible reusability*: the same data can be reclassified for different purposes endlessly. *Conditioning forces*: they shape outcomes far beyond their immediate function, by determining what becomes visible, knowable and actionable.

The authors then walk through three illustrative domains where datastructuring has visible effects. *Social*: the case of "spreading santorum," where coordinated linking redefined a US senator's name in Google's eyes. *Political*: the Cambridge Analytica / Trump campaign case, where data-based microtargeting wasn't just delivering messages but *conditioning* how messages were received. *Commercial*: news production, where datastructuring lets advertising-driven popularity displace journalistic judgment about importance, contributing to the rise of fake news.

The conclusion calls datastructures *epistemic architecture*: structured and structuring conditions that work as both conduits and barriers to information. In an age when "the data speak for themselves" is accepted as common sense, datastructuring is what we should study to understand why some things become known and others don't.

### Key concepts (with everyday-language definitions)

#### Datastructuring

The ongoing process by which data gets organised, sorted, tagged and made actionable, and through which it shapes what we see, know and do. Not just *what* data is collected, but *how* it gets arranged in a way that quietly steers our behaviour. It's meant as a verb-noun pair with *datastructures* (the configurations themselves). The point is that the form of the data, not the content, is doing a lot of the work.

*Why this matters for the exam:* this is the concept the paper exists to introduce. If I can define it clearly, I have the centre of the paper.

#### Datafication

The conversion of human activities into data that can be combined, analysed, and put to multiple uses. Every click, swipe, location ping and reaction emoji becomes a data point. The term comes from Mayer-Schönberger and Cukier, but Flyverbom uses it as the precondition for datastructuring. First activity becomes data, then that data gets structured.

*Why this matters for the exam:* I may be asked the difference between datafication and datastructuring. Datafication is the *raw material* (turning life into data); datastructuring is the *processing* (organising that data into actionable forms).

#### Algorithmic recognisability

Tarleton Gillespie's term, but Flyverbom relies on it heavily. Making your message recognisable to a search engine or recommendation algorithm so it gets surfaced. The "spreading santorum" case is the prototype: by linking thousands of webpages with a particular phrase, activists made that phrase into the top Google result for "santorum." Algorithmic recognisability is the new rhetoric. It isn't enough to say something. You have to say it in a way the algorithm can pick up.

*Why this matters for the exam:* a great example of how form trumps content. Bring it up if asked about the political consequences of platforms.

#### Epistemic architecture

The paper's conclusion uses this phrase to describe what datastructures are: structured and structuring conditions that work as conduits for and barriers to information. The building plan of what we can know. Calling them "architecture" is a deliberate move. It signals that they are designed, durable, and have politics, just like the architecture of a building.

*Why this matters for the exam:* it's the takeaway phrase. If I remember nothing else, I remember "datastructures as epistemic architecture."

#### Raw data is an oxymoron (Bowker)

Borrowed from Geoffrey Bowker, but the authors use it as the implicit motto of the paper. There is no such thing as raw data. By the time you encounter it, it has already been cooked. This is what the field usually calls "data critique." It's the polar opposite of the popular slogan "the data speak for themselves."

*Why this matters for the exam:* a great line to drop. It captures, in five words, why the paper exists.

### The example(s) the author uses

*Spreading santorum.* In 2003, US Senator Rick Santorum gave an interview comparing same-sex relationships to bestiality. The sex columnist Dan Savage asked his readers to vote on a new definition for the word "santorum" and then mobilised them to link to a website (`spreadingsantorum.org`) using the new definition. The result: Google's algorithm started ranking that page above Santorum's own page for the search term "santorum." Years later, the senator's own attempts to fight back had to operate within the same datastructure, by adding more links pointing the other way. The point is that the *political act* was no longer to articulate a position; it was to *structure data* such that the political position took algorithmic form. The form became the action.

*Cambridge Analytica and the 2016 US election.* The standard story is "Cambridge Analytica targeted voters with custom messages." Flyverbom and Murray push deeper. The actual mechanism, they argue, was *datastructuring*: data on voter profiles aggregated across platforms, used to construct discrete audiences, fed messages, then used the responses to construct *new* audiences (those who shared, those who clicked, those who showed hostility). Each iteration produced more refined targeting. On top of that, the message arrived in a *conditioned context*, accompanied by Twitter bot activity that made the message seem to have audience support. So voters weren't just being told something. They were being told something inside a manufactured environment of social proof. The persuasion came from form, not just content.

*News production and fake news.* News stories on the web aren't just digital copies of newspaper articles. Each story sits inside an ecosystem of 100–250 partner services contributing or extracting data. The systems that decide what readers see (what gets surfaced on Facebook, what shows up in Google News) work on coarse signals like clicks, shares, time-on-page. So commercially-minded publishers learn to feed the algorithm what it likes. Popularity displaces relevance. The infrastructure of news has changed in ways that make fake news economically rational, even if no human in the chain has decided to lie. This is datastructuring shaping epistemology.

### How this applies to my case (Publikum / arthouse / IMDb data)

The IMDb dataset is itself an example of datastructuring, and the paper helps me think about what that means.

*The data are not raw.* The 23-column dataset (`titleId`, `genres`, `keywords`, `plotShort`, etc.) is the output of layers of structuring choices made before I touched it. IMDb decided what counts as a genre. Users contribute keywords, but only the ones that were proposed and accepted. Plot summaries come in three lengths because someone decided three lengths was useful. The countries of production are coded in a particular schema. The "isAdult" flag exists because someone decided that distinction was relevant. The dataset is already datastructured before any analysis I do. Flyverbom and Murray's "raw data is an oxymoron" applies in full.

*My arthouse cohort is a new layer of datastructuring.* When my team chose `rule OR LLM ≥ 8` as the cohort definition, we created a new datastructure: a binary in/out classification overlaid on the IMDb ecosystem. Films that are "in" become visible to subsequent analysis (comparable-title search, market strategy questions). Films that are "out" become invisible. The choice of threshold (≥ 8) is not neutral. It conditions what Publikum can see. If we'd set the threshold at 7, the cohort would expand and include films that are now excluded. Different threshold, different epistemic architecture.

*Commercial datastructuring on streaming platforms.* The decision questions Publikum cares about (positioning, audiences, markets, comparables) are exactly the kind of questions a streaming platform optimises through datastructuring. Netflix's recommendations, Letterboxd's tags, Spotify-for-podcasts logic: these are commercial datastructures. When Publikum advises a film on positioning, it's effectively recommending a strategy for *making the film algorithmically recognisable* to streaming services and audience-segmentation tools. So the practical work of "positioning" in 2026 is increasingly the work of datastructuring.

*Risks for the project.* Flyverbom and Murray push me to be reflexive. The comparable-title model could *create* new datastructures that have ordering effects nobody anticipated. If we tell Publikum that a film is "comparable to" three Belgian-French co-productions, that classification might travel: into pitch decks, into festival applications, into the way distributors think about the film. The risk is that our model isn't just describing the world; it's *making* a world. This is the classic critical-data-studies move, and the paper gives me the vocabulary to articulate it: datastructures don't only disclose what is there. They are generative.

*The "audience-insights through AI-enhanced anthropology" pitch.* Publikum's marketing claim is that AI-enhanced anthropology can produce audience insights. Flyverbom and Murray would say: yes, it can, but those insights are conditioned by the datastructures the AI sits on. The plot summaries the LLM reads are themselves datastructured. The training data of the LLM is datastructured. The metadata schemas of IMDb are datastructured. Insights all the way down. The honest version of the pitch acknowledges this — and that's exactly what the framework asks for.

### A critical view

The biggest weakness of Flyverbom and Murray is that *datastructuring* is an umbrella concept rather than a tight one. By design, it pulls together infrastructure, platforms, and algorithms, and that gives it a kind of "everything everywhere" feel. A sceptical examiner might press: how do you operationalise this? How do you study datastructuring empirically without it just being "the world"? The paper itself acknowledges this is exploratory and offers illustrations rather than methods. So as a critique tool it's powerful; as a research programme it's still maturing. Second, the three illustrative cases (Santorum, Cambridge Analytica, news production) are all by 2018 well-known to the data-and-society field. The paper recognises this and uses them illustratively, but a critic could say it doesn't yet show what *new* analyses datastructuring opens up. Third, like a lot of critical-data-studies work, it identifies the problem (epistemic architecture is shaping us in invisible ways) more sharply than the solution (what to do about it). It moves us "away from the simplistic calls for algorithmic transparency" but doesn't replace them with a clear alternative. A strong answer would acknowledge these limits while still recognising the genuine power of the conceptual move.

### Two or three quotes worth knowing

> "Datastructuring may become more important than contents or social networks in strategic communication and related attempts to influence customers, publics, and politics." (p. 2)

> "Raw data is an oxymoron." (Bowker, quoted on p. 5)

> "We may think of [datastructures] as a novel and overlooked type of 'epistemic architecture' — structured and structuring conditions that work as conduits for and barriers to information." (p. 10)

### What an examiner is most likely to ask

**Q: What is datastructuring and why is it different from datafication?**
Datafication is the *conversion* of human activity into data. Every click, like, location ping becomes a data point. Datastructuring is what happens *next*: the practices through which that data gets organised, sorted, tagged, recommended, made actionable. Datafication produces the raw material; datastructuring is the cooking. The authors care about the cooking because they argue it has been understudied. Communication theory has focused on content (what messages say) and distribution (how they spread), but not on the structuring conditions that determine which messages can even appear.

**Q: Walk me through one of their three examples.**
Take "spreading santorum." A US senator made offensive comments about same-sex relationships in 2003. A columnist mobilised readers to link to a website giving "santorum" a new, vulgar definition. Through coordinated linking (what's called googlebombing) they made Google's algorithm rank the new page above the senator's own page. The political act was no longer just speaking; it was *structuring data* so that the political position took algorithmic form. Even the senator's response had to operate within that datastructure. This is the paper's point in miniature: form became action.

**Q: What are the four features of datastructures?**
Socio-material entanglement (always both technical and human), recursive informating (they fold new data back into themselves), flexible reusability (the same data can be reclassified endlessly), and conditioning forces (they shape outcomes far beyond their immediate function). Together, these four features make datastructures hard to study but powerful to live with.

**Q: How does this relate to "raw data is an oxymoron"?**
That phrase is from Geoffrey Bowker, but Flyverbom and Murray use it as the moral of the paper. There is no such thing as data that hasn't already been processed, classified, structured. By the time you see a number on a dashboard, that number has been through metadata schemas, sampling decisions, sorting algorithms, and platform rules. The popular slogan "let the data speak for itself" treats data as raw and neutral. Bowker's line, and the paper, push back: data always speaks in someone's grammar.

**Q: What is "epistemic architecture"?**
Datastructures as epistemic architecture means structured and structuring conditions that work as both conduits for and barriers to information, in the same way the architecture of a building shapes who can enter where. It's a deliberate move to call them architecture, because architecture is designed, durable, has politics. Flyverbom and Murray want us to study datastructures the way Foucault studied prisons: not just as containers, but as systems that shape what becomes knowable.

## 4. Galbraith (2014) — Big data is not just analytics, it's a redesign challenge

**Elevator summary.** Companies that try to "do big data" by simply hiring some data scientists usually fail. Jay Galbraith argues that big data forces three deep organisational changes. First, power has to shift from experienced judgmental decision-makers to analytics experts, and that's painful. Think of the Moneyball scene where the old scouts won't let the data guy into the meeting. Second, decisions have to speed up to real time. Galbraith calls this increasing the "clock speed" of the organisation, with newsroom-style cross-functional teams. Third, the data itself can become a new business. The analytics insights you generate for yourself can be sold to others, opening up new revenue streams. Nike's Digital Sports Division is his running case study. The paper ends with the Star Model framework (strategy, structure, processes, rewards, people) to show that all five elements have to change in concert.

### The big argument

Galbraith starts by being honest: big data is hyped. But underneath the hype there is real substance. What's actually new about it? Two things. First, the data is more diverse: not just structured rows and columns from transactions, but unstructured stuff like photos, videos, tweets, GPS pings, voice recordings. Second, and more importantly, it's *real-time*. Events can be observed and acted on as they happen, not analysed historically.

The real-time nature is what triggers organisational redesign. Old organisations were built for batch processing: quarterly reviews, weekly meetings, monthly reports. If your data arrives in real time, your organisation has to act in real time, or the value evaporates. Real-time data plus a slow organisation is wasted capital. So big data's first organisational challenge is *clock speed*.

The second challenge is *power shift*. Every organisation has an establishment: the people currently making the calls based on years of experience. Big data introduces a new class of decision-maker: data scientists, analytics experts, digital folks. There will be friction. Galbraith uses the Moneyball example. The old scouts, with their grey hair and gut feeling, refuse to let the 25-year-old Yale economist into the meeting. The lesson is that the leader has to actively support the new guard, or they'll be marginalised by the establishment. Galbraith goes through several examples: P&G, where big data is competence-*enhancing* (the company already valued analytics, so adoption is smooth); risk management in financial services, where Chief Risk Officers were sidelined as the "revenue reduction department" before the 2008 crisis; and various Chief Digital Officer roles (P&G's CIO doubling up, Intel's two-managers-in-a-box, Starbucks's full-time CDO, IBM's Enterprise Transformation Head).

The third challenge, and the most interesting one, is that big data can become a *new business*. The data and analytics generated for internal decision-making can be packaged and sold. Galbraith's examples: JP Morgan and Wells Fargo selling consumer-trend reports built on transaction data; Citibank selling international consumer insights to companies like Zara and H&M for store-location decisions; Bosch turning its automotive sensor data into a software business. And Nike, the running case, taking its NikePlus.com community data and turning it into the Digital Sports Division: a fifth dimension of the org chart, complete with its own products (Nike+ FuelBand), its own ecosystem (the incubator for running apps), and its own profit centre.

The paper closes with the Star Model. Strategy, structure, processes, rewards, and people all have to be aligned for big data to land. Pick a digital strategy. Build a digital structure (dedicated unit, governance committee). Redesign processes for real-time decision-making (cross-functional newsrooms, decision spheres). Adjust rewards to value digital and analytical performance. Hire and train people who are quantitatively comfortable. The change isn't a single hire. It's a redesign of all five.

### Key concepts (with everyday-language definitions)

#### Power shift

When big data analytics enters a company, decision-making power has to move from the old experienced judgment-makers to the new analytics experts. Grandpa with 30 years of experience used to call the shots. Now Grandpa has to listen to the 25-year-old data scientist. The transition is messy and political. If the leader doesn't actively back the new players, the establishment will marginalise them.

*Why this matters for the exam:* a memorable concept with the Moneyball reference attached. If asked about the *organisational* (not technical) challenge of big data, this is my lead answer.

#### Competence-enhancing vs competence-destroying

These are Tushman and Anderson's terms, used by Galbraith. A new technology can either *make existing skills more valuable* (competence-enhancing) or *make existing skills obsolete* (competence-destroying). The first is easy to adopt; the second creates resistance. E-commerce was competence-enhancing for Dell (which already sold direct) but competence-destroying for HP (which depended on resellers). Galbraith's lesson: figure out which side of the line you're on, because the resistance you'll face depends on it.

*Why this matters for the exam:* a useful diagnostic frame for any "should we adopt X technology?" question.

#### Clock speed

Galbraith's metaphor for the speed of organisational decision-making. How fast does the organisation tick? Real-time data needs a real-time organisation. Just as computer designers have steadily raised the clock speed of processors, organisation designers have to raise the clock speed of their organisations. Newsroom-style ad teams that respond to social media in minutes. Decision spheres at P&G where supply chain decisions are made in real time. The unit of organisational design becomes faster cycles.

*Why this matters for the exam:* clock speed is the concept that connects "big data is real-time" to "we need to redesign our processes." It's the bridge.

#### Chief Digital Officer (CDO)

The executive responsible for embedding big data analytics across the organisation. A new C-suite role to make sure the analytics actually gets used, not just generated. Galbraith argues that the form depends on the situation. If big data is competence-enhancing (P&G), a CIO doubling up may be enough. If it's competence-destroying (a big bank, an old industrial firm), you need something stronger, like IBM's Enterprise Transformation Head, with the CIO and process design reporting up.

*Why this matters for the exam:* if asked what role to create, the answer is "depends on whether it's enhancing or destroying."

#### Star Model

Galbraith's classic framework. An organisation is held together by alignment across five elements: strategy, structure, processes, rewards, people. Change one without changing the others, and you'll fail. Big data forces change in all five.

*Why this matters for the exam:* the Star Model is Galbraith's signature framework. If asked to summarise the impact of big data on organisations, this is the clean way to do it.

### The example(s) the author uses

*Procter & Gamble — competence-enhancing.* P&G has had an analytics group since 1992, will try anything to understand consumer behaviour, and exchanges teams with Google annually. Big data is congenial. The CIO plays the CDO role. 88 business processes are being redesigned for real time. Performance management evaluates digital and analytical capability. P&G has decision spheres: special rooms with screens and database access, where cross-functional teams make supply-chain decisions in real time. When a Pampers paper machine in Wisconsin signals it needs maintenance, the team in the decision sphere reroutes Wal-Mart supply from the Albany, Georgia plant. Real-time at the operational level.

*The 2013 Super Bowl — newsroom advertising.* Coca-Cola and Audi gathered rapid-response teams on game day. When the infamous power outage hit the Mercedes-Benz Superdome, Audi tweeted "Sending some LEDs to the MBUSA Superdome right now," within minutes, plugging its own LED-lit cars at the expense of its rival. This is what Galbraith calls the shift from *campaign* model (plan ads in September for February broadcast) to *newsroom* model (prepare to respond in real time).

*Old Spice — real-time social media response.* The "man on the horse" ad went viral. The agency assembled a large team (writers, art directors, producers, editors, the actor, social media specialists) and over two days created 200 humorous video responses to comments from viewers, several per hour. The team became a real-time content factory.

*Nike — the running thread.* Galbraith's book-length case. In 1998 Nike launched nike.com. In 2005 NIKEiD let customers design their own shoes. Then came Nike+: a sensor in the shoe, syncing with the iPod, then the iPhone, then NikePlus.com. By 2013 the Nike+ community had over seven million members. A 24/7 social-network team monitors conversations, runs sentiment analysis, swings into action when there's a positive or negative spike. They're a newsroom too. New products emerged (Nike+ FuelBand, GPS SportWatch), and Nike began competing in wearable medical devices. In 2010 Nike created a Digital Sports Division as a new profit centre. By 2013 the org chart had five dimensions: function, region, product, customer category, *digital*.

*JP Morgan, Wells Fargo, Citibank — data as a new business.* Banks have vast transactional data. They combine it with public data, analyse it, and sell the resulting insights as customised reports: to retailers wanting demographic profiles, to companies like Zara and H&M wanting to know where to put new stores, to anyone willing to buy. The data and analytics that started as internal decision support become external revenue streams.

### How this applies to my case (Publikum / arthouse / IMDb data)

Galbraith's framework is a useful diagnostic for thinking about Publikum as an organisation, even though my role is on the analyst side rather than the leadership side.

*Power shift in miniature.* My team is, in Galbraith's terms, the analytics newcomers in Publikum's ecosystem. Publikum has its own established judgement-makers: film consultants, AI-anthropologists, account managers who know the industry. When we deliver an arthouse cohort definition or a comparable-title model, we're walking into a Moneyball scene. The film consultants might say, "I've worked with arthouse for fifteen years, and that's not how we define it." Galbraith's lesson: success depends on whether Publikum's leadership actively backs the data side or treats us as the "revenue reduction department." So part of our communication strategy has to be about getting executive sponsorship, not just delivering charts.

*Competence-enhancing or destroying?* Publikum sells "AI-enhanced anthropology." That positioning suggests big data is competence-*enhancing* for them. They've already framed AI as part of their value proposition. So the structural shift should be smoother than at, say, a traditional film distributor. But there's a subtlety: within Publikum, our specific use of *generative LLMs to score films* may still be competence-destroying for the human anthropologists. If the LLM rates a film as "8 — clearly arthouse," does that displace the anthropologist's judgment? Galbraith would say: figure out where the friction is, and design for it.

*Clock speed.* Publikum's decision questions (positioning, audiences, markets, comparables) are not really *real-time* questions. A film positioning decision is made over weeks or months, not minutes. So the clock-speed pressure that drove Nike's newsroom isn't directly applicable. But there is a softer version: the speed at which Publikum can answer a *new* question. If a producer asks "how does my film compare to Belgian-French co-productions?" today, can Publikum produce an answer within 48 hours, or does it take six weeks? The notebook plus enrichment pipeline I'm building is essentially a clock-speed upgrade. It moves Publikum from a slow, custom-research model to a fast, query-the-system model.

*Data as a potential new business.* This is the most interesting Galbraith move for Publikum. The 2,000-film dataset, plus the arthouse cohort, plus the comparable-title engine, could itself be a *product*. Publikum could sell access to this analysis layer to producers, distributors, and festivals, the way Citibank sells consumer insights to Zara. The Digital Sports Division equivalent might be "Publikum Insights as a Service." That's not what Publikum does today, but it's the direction Galbraith would point.

*Star Model in our team.* Even within our six-person student team, the Star Model maps. Strategy: deliver actionable recommendations honest about limits. Structure: how the team divides work between cohort definition, EDA, network analysis, comparable-title engine. Processes: how we move from notebook to slides. Rewards: this is school, so the reward is grade and learning, but the principle holds. Does our team value analytical rigor or storytelling more, and does that match the strategy? People: who has Python, who has writing chops, who has design? Misalignment in any of these will show in the deliverable.

### A critical view

Galbraith's framework is now over a decade old, and the world it describes (big data as a *novel* phenomenon in 2014) is no longer the world. Today, "having an analytics capability" is table stakes. The conversation has moved to AI agents, foundation models, and platform power. Galbraith's recommendations (hire a CDO, redesign processes) feel obvious in 2026 in a way they didn't in 2014. Second, the paper is unapologetically managerialist. It assumes the goal is firm performance and competitive advantage. It doesn't engage with the labour, ethics, or epistemic concerns that the rest of the week's readings raise. The Kenyan content moderators don't appear. The Cambridge Analytica scandal doesn't appear. The question of whose data is being repackaged for resale doesn't appear. Citibank selling Zara consumer insights is presented as a positive, not as a privacy or surveillance issue. Read alongside Justesen & Plesner or Flyverbom & Murray, Galbraith's take feels naive about who pays the costs. Third, the Nike case is now dated. The wearable-fitness boom has cooled, FuelBand was discontinued, the Digital Sports Division was reorganised. So the empirical anchor doesn't quite hold up. A strong answer should use Galbraith for the structural insights (Star Model, power shift, clock speed) but situate them inside the more critical readings.

### Two or three quotes worth knowing

> "Big data is not just a new revenue source; it may be the start of a new organizational dimension." (p. 10)

> "The arrival of data and analytics at the Oakland Athletics destroyed or diminished the experience-based competence of Grady and his fellow talent scouts." (p. 4)

> "The amount of power and authority of a CDO should be matched with the relative amount of difficulty and priority of implementing big data." (p. 4)

### What an examiner is most likely to ask

**Q: According to Galbraith, what are the three main organisational challenges of big data?**
First, a power shift from experienced judgmental decision-makers to analytics experts (the Moneyball problem). Second, an increase in clock speed: the organisation has to move to real-time decision-making, with cross-functional newsroom-style teams and decision spheres. Third, big data can itself become a new business. The data and analytics produced for internal use can be packaged and sold, opening a new revenue dimension. Nike's Digital Sports Division is the case study tying all three together.

**Q: What's the difference between competence-enhancing and competence-destroying big data?**
Competence-enhancing means the new analytics capability *makes existing skills more valuable*. P&G is the example: already an analytical company, big data fits right in, resistance is low. Competence-destroying means the new capability *makes existing skills obsolete*. Big data in baseball scouting is the example: the old scouts' experiential expertise is undermined by the data, and they resist. Galbraith's diagnostic: identify which side of the line your organisation is on, because the level of CDO authority and structural change you need depends on the resistance you'll face.

**Q: Walk me through the Nike example.**
Nike was a single-business shoe company in the 1960s. By the 2000s it had become a multi-dimensional matrix: function, region, product line, customer category. In 2005 it launched NIKEiD, an online customisation site. Then came Nike+, with a sensor in the shoe linking to iPods, then iPhones. NikePlus.com became the largest online running destination, with seven million members by 2013. New products emerged: FuelBand, SportWatch. In 2010 Nike created the Digital Sports Division as a new profit centre. The community generates data, the data drives new products, the products feed the community. This is Galbraith's full theory in one company: power shifted to digital, decisions sped up to real time (a 24/7 social media team), and digital became its own business unit.

**Q: What is the Star Model and why does Galbraith use it here?**
The Star Model is Galbraith's classic framework for organisational design: strategy, structure, processes, rewards, people. The point is that all five must align. Big data isn't just a hiring decision; it requires changes across all five. Strategy: choose what you're using big data for. Structure: create digital units and a CDO. Processes: redesign for real time. Rewards: evaluate digital and analytical performance. People: hire data scientists, train managers in quantitative methods. Galbraith uses it as a checklist. If your big-data initiative is failing, it's because one of the five isn't aligned with the others.

**Q: How does Galbraith's view differ from Justesen & Plesner's?**
Galbraith is managerial and optimistic. He looks at big data as an opportunity to redesign for competitive advantage. Justesen and Plesner are critical and structural. They look at the same digital infrastructure and ask who pays the cost. So Galbraith celebrates Citibank selling insights to Zara as a new revenue dimension. Justesen and Plesner would ask who's doing the data-cleaning labour that makes those insights possible, and whether they're paid for it. The two papers describe the same world from different sides. A complete view needs both.

## 5. Mützel (2025) — Big data and machine learning methods for studying innovation

**Elevator summary.** This is a methods chapter for innovation researchers. Mützel walks through the family of computational tools that have transformed how we study innovation: topic modelling for finding latent themes in large text corpora, word embeddings for mapping how concepts relate in conceptual space, and the new wave of large language models like BERT and GPT for nuanced understanding and even text generation. Each method has strengths and serious limits. The bigger argument is that *innovation* is fundamentally about recombining existing elements into something new, that newness has to be recognised and labelled and made legitimate by audiences, and that all this happens in stories. Which is why textual data and computational text analysis have become central to studying it. The chapter ends with a careful warning: these tools are research assistants, not replacements for human expertise.

### The big argument

Mützel begins with a definition of innovation she keeps returning to: *innovation is not spontaneous invention; it is the recombination of existing elements transformed into newness*. This is borrowed from Padgett and Powell. The key implications: innovation processes are non-linear (they fail, they restart, they change direction), newness needs to be *recognised* and *interpreted* by diverse actors, and that recognition usually involves *categorisation* — giving the new thing a label that audiences can understand. Smartphones, SUVs, "modern architecture," "molecular gastronomy" all became real innovations partly because someone settled on a label that stuck.

Because innovation lives in stories (patents, scientific papers, news articles, restaurant reviews, shareholder letters), text is the natural data for studying it. Until recently, researchers studied innovation through laborious hand-coding, qualitative interviews, and ethnography. The arrival of digitised and "born-digital" data changed the scale: instead of small samples, you can now work with "all the data." But that abundance demands new methods.

The chapter then walks through three generations of computational text analysis.

*Topic modelling (LDA).* Unsupervised. You feed the algorithm a corpus and tell it how many topics to find. It returns groups of words that frequently co-occur, and a distribution of topics across documents. It's exploratory and bottom-up, like grounded theory. The catch: it treats documents as "bags of words" (no syntax, no order), it can produce cryptic outputs that the researcher has to interpret, and it suffers from "interpretive uncertainty." Mützel's own work used topic modelling to trace the emergence of "targeted therapeutics" as a new breast cancer category.

*Word embeddings (Word2Vec).* Vector representations of words: each word becomes a point in a high-dimensional space, where words used in similar contexts sit close together. This lets you measure distance between concepts, trace semantic change over time, and detect biases (because the embeddings reflect the texts they were trained on, including stereotyped associations). Aceves and Evans' notion of "conceptual spaces" is the key idea: a multidimensional space in which norms, knowledge, ideas and inventions relate. The catch: word embeddings give static meanings. They can't handle polysemy (a word with multiple meanings, like "bank") or word order.

*Contextual embeddings (BERT) and generative LLMs (GPT family).* These are the current frontier. BERT looks at the words *before and after* a target word to determine its meaning in context, so it can finally distinguish "river bank" from "investment bank." It's used for fine-tuned tasks like text classification, hate speech detection, identifying rhetorical styles. Generative LLMs (GPT, Claude, Gemini) go further: they don't just classify, they generate. They can summarise, translate, draft, code, and act as research assistants. Studies show they can match or beat human annotators on coding tasks. But, and this is a big but, they hallucinate, they have proprietary closed-model versions that can't be peer-reviewed, and they encode training-data biases.

Throughout, Mützel insists these are tools, not replacements. The careful mantra: "We are now augmented social scientists." The methods extend human capability. They don't substitute for human expertise in interpretation, validation, and ethical judgment.

### Key concepts (with everyday-language definitions)

#### Innovation as recombination

Innovation is not invention from nothing. It is the rearrangement of existing elements into something new. Nothing comes from nothing. Every "new" thing is a recombination of older parts into a fresh pattern. This is the foundational claim. It means studying innovation is studying *which existing pieces* got brought together, *how* they got combined, and *who* recognised the combination as new.

*Why this matters for the exam:* if asked what Mützel means by innovation, lead with this.

#### Categorisation processes

For an innovation to spread, it has to be *named* and *placed in a category* that audiences can understand. A new product without a label is just confusion. A label like "smartphone" or "SUV" gives people something to attach meaning to. Stark's "reflexive cognition" breaks this into three moments: recognition (someone notices something new), labelling and categorisation (the new thing gets a name), and audience uptake (others come to value and support the new category). Categories may *emerge* (the thing exists, then gets named) or be *created* (actors define a new category by contrasting it with an existing one).

*Why this matters for the exam:* this is what justifies using textual analysis. Innovations live in the stories that label and legitimate them.

#### Topic modelling (LDA)

An unsupervised machine-learning method that finds groups of words that co-occur frequently in a corpus, treating each group as a "topic." Feed it 30,000 documents, ask for 200 topics, get back 200 word-clusters that are *probably* meaningful themes. It's inductive and exploratory, like grounded theory done by a computer. The researcher has to interpret what each cluster means. The famous limit: LDA is a "bag of words" model. It ignores syntax and word order.

*Why this matters for the exam:* topic modelling is the workhorse of computational social science of innovation. Be ready to explain what it does, what it ignores, and where it can fail.

#### Word embeddings

A method for turning words into numerical vectors such that words used in similar contexts end up close together in vector space. A way of mathematically representing the meaning of a word based on the company it keeps. The famous trick: you can do arithmetic on words. (King − Man + Woman ≈ Queen, in a well-trained embedding.) For innovation studies, word embeddings let you measure how *novel* a concept is by looking at where it sits in conceptual space.

*Why this matters for the exam:* the second-generation method. If asked about "geometry of meaning" or "conceptual spaces," this is it.

#### BERT (and contextual embeddings)

Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers. A model that, unlike Word2Vec, looks at the words on *both sides* of a target word to determine its meaning in context. BERT can finally distinguish the word "bank" in "river bank" from "bank" in "investment bank." It can be fine-tuned for specific tasks: classification, hate-speech detection, identifying rhetorical styles.

*Why this matters for the exam:* BERT is the bridge between the older static embeddings and the newer generative LLMs. If asked about what changed with transformer models, this is the answer.

#### Generative LLMs as augmented research assistants

The current wave (GPT, Claude, Gemini) doesn't just classify. It generates. Chatbots that can summarise, translate, code, draft propositions, even mimic understanding by predicting the next word. Mützel is enthusiastic but cautious. They're game-changers because they can scale qualitative work to enormous corpora. They're dangerous because they hallucinate, they encode training-data biases, and the proprietary closed versions can't be peer-reviewed. The mantra: "augmented social scientists" — humans plus machines, with humans doing the interpretation and validation.

*Why this matters for the exam:* this is where the field is in 2026. If asked about LLMs in research, this is the careful, balanced position.

### The example(s) the author uses

*Targeted therapeutics in breast cancer (Mützel 2022).* Mützel ran a 200-topic LDA on roughly 31,000 Web of Science abstracts (1990–2010) about breast cancer therapy. The analysis traced the emergence of a new category, "targeted therapeutics," as scientists, industry analysts, journalists, and companies all gradually converged on the label. Topic modelling let her see the discursive landscape across thousands of documents in a way no hand-coding team could have done.

*Letters to shareholders (Jung et al. 2024).* Word embeddings extracted the vocabulary CEOs used around "innovation" and let researchers see how the meaning of innovation differed across firms.

*Discursive diversity in software teams (Lix et al. 2022).* Word embeddings tracked how broadly or narrowly team members engaged with concepts at different stages of a project. High-performing teams adjusted their conceptual breadth: wide when generating ideas, narrow when coordinating.

*Patent novelty (Hofstra et al. 2020).* Word embeddings on a million dissertation abstracts let researchers measure where new concepts sit in the existing semantic network, and so quantify innovation novelty.

*Ethics codes evolving (Santana & Kim 2025).* Topic modelling and semantic networks tracked how contested values shift in codes of ethics over time.

*Hate speech detection (Davidson 2025).* Fine-tuned BERT models distinguish hate speech from non-hate speech with state-of-the-art accuracy.

*ChatGPT outperforming Mechanical Turk on annotation (Gilardi et al. 2023).* Instructed ChatGPT produced more accurate tweet-and-newspaper annotations than hired workers given the same instructions. This is the data point that justifies using LLMs as research assistants.

### How this applies to my case (Publikum / arthouse / IMDb data)

Mützel is the most directly methodological reading of the week, and the closest to what I'm actually doing. Several of her concepts map onto the project at the level of method choice.

*Innovation as recombination, applied to films.* If "arthouse" is itself an emergent category, then studying it follows Mützel's logic. The films my team is sorting into the arthouse cohort are recombinations of existing elements: genre conventions, national cinema traditions, festival circuits, casting choices. The category "arthouse" is itself a label that has emerged over time and is contested. Defining who is in and who is out is a *categorisation* exercise in Mützel's sense. So the project isn't just classification. It's participation in an ongoing categorisation process.

*The hybrid arthouse rule as augmented research.* Our use of the LLM to score films is a textbook case of Mützel's "augmented social scientist" mantra. The hand-built rule encodes human expert judgment. The LLM score brings scaled language understanding. The OR combination is the explicit augmentation: human plus machine, neither replacing the other. This is Mützel's recommended pattern.

*Topic modelling and the genre/keyword data.* The IMDb dataset has rich textual fields: `keywords`, `plotShort`, `plotMedium`, `plotLong`. A natural extension of the project is to run topic modelling on plot summaries to discover *latent thematic clusters* among the 2,000 European films. This would give Publikum a different lens than genre alone. For example, "intimate family drama" and "political thriller about identity" might cut across IMDb's official genre tags. Mützel's chapter is the methodological warrant for doing that.

*Word embeddings for comparable-title analysis.* The current comparable-title module in `src/analysis.py` likely uses some version of textual or feature-based similarity. If we used word embeddings on plot summaries (or sentence embeddings via BERT), we'd get a "geometry of meaning" specifically for the European film space. Aceves and Evans' "conceptual space" idea — concepts relating to one another in a multidimensional space — is exactly what comparable-title analysis tries to operationalise. Mützel's chapter is the academic framing for this method choice.

*BERT-style classification of arthouse vs mainstream.* Instead of (or alongside) the LLM scoring, a fine-tuned BERT classifier on plot summaries could give a probabilistic arthouse-ness score. This is competitive with the LLM approach and arguably more transparent, since you can inspect the model. The LLM gives a number and a paragraph; BERT gives a number and a learned representation. Both are tools for the same task.

*Limits I have to be honest about.* Mützel's warnings apply directly. The IMDb plot summaries are not balanced. English-language films get longer, more detailed plots; non-English films are often summarised through translation and may carry biases. Whatever topic model or embedding I run will reflect those biases. The LLM scoring may "hallucinate," assigning arthouse to a film for the wrong reasons. Mützel's chapter gives me the language to surface these risks in the final write-up.

*The sociology of innovation isn't far from the sociology of film categorisation.* Mützel studies how new categories emerge in markets. Publikum is in the business of helping films find audience categories. The intellectual continuity is striking: my comparable-title model is producing categorisation suggestions for an industry that is itself in the middle of categorisation processes (festival circuits, streaming taxonomies, marketing labels). I am, in a small way, participating in the same kind of process Mützel studies.

### A critical view

Mützel's chapter is a clean, well-organised methods overview, but it has the limits of genre. First, it's a chapter in a handbook, so it's surveying the state of the art rather than making a sharp original argument. The reading is informative but not provocative. An examiner might ask "what is Mützel's *thesis*?" and the honest answer is that she doesn't have one in the strong sense. She has a careful position (use these tools, augment human expertise, beware the limits) rather than a contentious claim. Second, the chapter is Eurocentric and English-centric in its examples. Almost all of the case studies are on English-language corpora. The bias of the methods toward English is acknowledged but not deeply addressed. For my project, working with European multi-language films, this is more than a footnote. Third, the chapter underplays the ethical questions about LLMs. Mützel mentions hallucinations, biases, and proprietary models, but the political economy of LLMs (who owns them, who pays for the training, who labels the toxic content — the very topic of Justesen and Plesner) gets only glancing treatment. Read alongside the rest of the week, the methods chapter feels almost too clean. Finally, the chapter treats "innovation research" as the default frame, but the same methods are applicable far beyond innovation, and a more capacious framing would have helped students like me who aren't doing strict innovation studies.

> "Innovation is not spontaneous invention. Rather, it is the recombination of existing elements transformed into 'newness.'" (Section 2)

> "Computational tools are complements to and not substitutes for human expertise. → Consider them as assistants. 'We are now augmented social scientists.'" (lecture slides)

> "The use of LLMs… enables social scientists 'to examine corpora of unprecedented size with unforeseen speed.'" (quoting Bail 2024)

### What an examiner is most likely to ask

**Q: How does Mützel define innovation?**
Innovation is the recombination of existing elements transformed into newness. It's not spontaneous invention from nothing. It's rearranging old pieces into new patterns. This definition is foundational because it implies that studying innovation is studying *which* pieces got brought together, *how*, *by whom*, and *who recognised the combination as new*. The non-linearity matters too. Innovations involve failures, restarts, contested interpretations. So the empirical study of innovation has to be process-oriented and longitudinal.

**Q: Walk me through topic modelling.**
Topic modelling, specifically LDA, is an unsupervised machine-learning method. You give it a corpus of documents and tell it how many topics to find. It returns clusters of words that frequently co-occur, and a distribution of topics across documents. It's inductive. Little a priori is required, no dictionaries. The researcher then has to interpret what each topic means substantively. Mützel calls this "interpretive uncertainty." The famous limit is that LDA treats documents as bags of words: no syntax, no word order. That's a real loss, but it's the price of tractability at scale.

**Q: How are word embeddings different from topic modelling?**
Topic modelling clusters *documents* by the words they contain. Word embeddings represent *individual words* as vectors in a high-dimensional space. So topic modelling tells you "these documents are about X." Word embeddings tell you "these two words mean similar things." Word embeddings let you measure semantic distance between concepts, trace meaning change over time, and detect bias. The big limit of static embeddings like Word2Vec is that they can't handle polysemy. "Bank" gets a single vector even though it has multiple meanings. That's where contextual embeddings like BERT come in.

**Q: What's new about BERT and GPT models for research?**
BERT is bidirectional. It looks at the words before *and* after a target word to determine context. So it can finally distinguish "river bank" from "investment bank." That makes it powerful for fine-tuned tasks: classification, hate-speech detection, identifying rhetorical styles. GPT goes further. Instead of classifying, it generates. It can summarise, translate, draft text, even mimic understanding. Studies like Gilardi et al. (2023) show GPT can match or beat human coders on annotation tasks. So they're "game-changers" — but with serious caveats: hallucinations, proprietary closed models, encoded biases, reproducibility problems. Mützel's mantra is "augmented social scientists." Use these tools, but don't let them replace human interpretation and validation.

**Q: When would you choose topic modelling over LLM-based annotation?**
If I want exploration ("what themes are in this corpus?"), I'd use topic modelling, because it's bottom-up and inductive. It can surface patterns I didn't know to look for. If I have a *specific* coding scheme and I want to apply it at scale ("does this article contain hate speech?", "does this film fit the arthouse category?"), I'd use a fine-tuned BERT or an instructed LLM, because they're better at deductive classification. The two are complements: I might use LDA to discover the latent themes in the IMDb plot summaries, then use an LLM to score each film against the themes. Mützel's chapter is exactly this kind of decision tree.

## Connecting the readings

These six readings (counting the lecture slides as a sixth) hang together as a single argument about technology, data, and organisations, even though they come from very different intellectual neighbourhoods. The argument is roughly this: information technology has stopped being a tool that organisations use and has become a constitutive part of how organisations exist, and that fact has consequences the field has only begun to take seriously.

Zammuto sets the agenda. Hierarchy used to be the main thread of organisational fabric. IT is now another thread. The right way to think about this is through *affordances* (what new combinations of technology and organisational features make possible), rather than through old categories like "structure" or "form." That move opens the door to everything else in the week.

Justesen and Plesner take Zammuto's affordances seriously but ask the question he doesn't: who is doing the work that holds the affordances together? The answer is invisible digi-workers, doing the connecting, compensating, and cleaning that the rhetoric of seamless automation pretends doesn't happen. Where Zammuto sees a new fabric being woven, they want us to also notice who is sweeping the floor. The two papers don't contradict each other so much as describe the same thing from opposite sides.

Flyverbom and Murray go a layer deeper. Before any of the affordances or the labour, data is being structured: sorted, ranked, tagged, made algorithmically recognisable. That datastructuring is itself a form of social action. It conditions what becomes visible and knowable. If Zammuto is about affordances and Justesen and Plesner are about the labour underneath affordances, Flyverbom and Murray are about the epistemic infrastructure that determines what can become an affordance in the first place.

Galbraith comes from a different tradition (the practical, managerial side of organisation design), but his argument fits inside the same picture. He says big data forces three redesign moves: power shift to analytics, clock-speed increase, new business creation. Read alongside Zammuto, Galbraith is operationalising the affordance argument. Here's what you actually have to do to realise the affordance. Read alongside Justesen and Plesner, Galbraith looks managerially optimistic. He doesn't see the labour costs. Read alongside Flyverbom and Murray, he doesn't see the epistemic costs. So Galbraith is a useful frame for what *to do*, but the critical readings are needed to see what gets hidden when you do it.

Mützel's chapter and slides shift register. They are methodological. They ask: now that we want to study these phenomena (organisations, innovations, categorisations) computationally, what tools do we have? Topic modelling, word embeddings, BERT, generative LLMs. The key takeaway connects back to the rest of the week: the tools are powerful but they encode the same biases and labour costs that the other papers warn about. The "augmented social scientist" stance is a reflexive one. Use the tools, but don't lose sight of who built them, who labels their training data, and what their outputs hide.

The week's overarching argument, then, is something like this: *Technology and organisations co-produce each other (as the Winthereik lecture puts it), but that co-production is built on invisible labour, structured by hidden epistemic architectures, and methodologically studied through tools that are themselves products of the same processes.* Each paper opens one black box. Together, they remind us that there is no "view from nowhere" of the digitalised organisation. Only situated views from particular positions, each of which sees some things and hides others. The way to do honest analytics is to keep moving between positions, to keep asking what each lens can't see.

For the Publikum project specifically, the week's collective lesson is: the arthouse cohort definition, the comparable-title model, and the four decision questions are *not just analytical outputs*. They are affordances built on invisible work, conditioned by datastructures, demanding redesign of how Publikum makes decisions, and produced by methods that have their own biases. The pitch and presentation should reflect all four readings, not just the upbeat managerial framing.

## Likely cross-reading exam questions

**Q1: How would Zammuto's affordance lens read Justesen and Plesner's invisible work?**
Zammuto says affordances arise when technological features and organisational features combine. Justesen and Plesner add that some of those organisational features are themselves *invisible digi-work*: connecting, compensating, and cleaning that don't show up in any org chart. So an honest application of the affordance lens has to count invisible work as one of the organisational features. The "visualising entire work processes" affordance, for instance, only exists because someone is cleaning the data the dashboard displays, compensating for missing fields, and connecting the database to the visualisation layer. Without the digi-work, the affordance evaporates. So the two papers complement each other. Zammuto names the lens; Justesen and Plesner show what the lens has to actually include.

**Q2: How does Flyverbom and Murray's "datastructuring" relate to Galbraith's "big data redesign"?**
Galbraith treats big data as a strategic resource and tells managers how to redesign their organisations to extract value from it. Flyverbom and Murray go one level deeper: before that data can be treated as a resource, it has been structured, sorted, and made actionable through hidden processes. So Galbraith is operating at the level of "we have data, what do we do with it?" while Flyverbom and Murray are operating at the level of "how is the data already shaped before we even get it?" The two papers are complementary but politically different. Galbraith is a managerial guide. Flyverbom and Murray are a critical caution. A manager reading both should still redesign for analytics, but should also be reflexive about what the data is already doing to the organisation before any decision is made.

**Q3: Pick one of Zammuto's affordances and apply it to your project, making sure to flag its invisible-work and datastructuring costs.**
Take the "visualising entire work processes" affordance. In our Publikum project, the EDA notebook with 18 figures is the visualisation. The technological features include pandas dataframes, matplotlib, and the IMDb dataset itself. The organisational features include our team's process for deciding what to plot, our reward structure (grades and learning), our weekly meetings. Read through Zammuto, the affordance is real. The team can see the whole 2,000-film space at once. Read through Justesen and Plesner, the affordance depends on hours of invisible cleaning work — parsing comma-separated fields, handling missing values, dealing with the IMDb GraphQL rate limits. Read through Flyverbom and Murray, the visualisation rests on a series of datastructuring choices: IMDb's genre taxonomy, the comma-separated coding of countries, the three-length plot summary schema. Each layer is a structuring choice that shapes what the dashboard can show. So when I present the dashboard to Publikum, the honest thing to do is to acknowledge all three layers (the affordance, the labour, and the datastructure) rather than pretend the visualisation is just "what the data shows."

**Q4: How does Mützel's notion of innovation as recombination connect to Zammuto's notion of affordances?**
Both rest on the idea that something genuinely new emerges from combining existing elements. Zammuto's affordances are combinations of existing technological features and existing organisational features, and what makes them powerful is that the combination produces a possibility neither half had alone. Mützel's innovations are combinations of existing knowledge, practices, and people, and what makes them new is that the combination is recognised, labelled, and legitimated by audiences. The structural logic is the same: innovation/affordance is recombination plus recognition. Where Mützel adds something is on the recognition side. She insists that without categorisation and labelling, the new thing doesn't take hold socially. Zammuto is mostly silent on this; he assumes affordances are realised when actors intend to use them. Read together, you could say: Mützel gives a sociology of how new affordances get *legitimated*, while Zammuto gives a typology of what new affordances *look like*.

**Q5: Suppose Publikum's CEO read all four critical/methodological readings (Zammuto, Justesen & Plesner, Flyverbom & Murray, Galbraith). What recommendations would each give for the company's strategy?**
Zammuto would recommend mapping the company's current and potential affordances. What new ways of organising are now possible because of AI-enhanced anthropology and big data, and what organisational features (process, culture, structure) are needed to realise them? He'd warn against treating data analytics as plumbing; it is a thread of the fabric. Justesen and Plesner would recommend a labour audit. Who in Publikum is doing the connecting, compensating, and cleaning that the smooth client deliverables depend on? Are they recognised, paid, and supported, or are they being self-devalued and burnt out? They'd also recommend explicit communication of the invisible work to clients, as part of being transparent about how the insights are produced. Flyverbom and Murray would recommend reflexivity about Publikum's own datastructuring. What classifications, taxonomies, and rankings is the company producing and selling, and what ordering effects are those classifications having on the film industry? Are we aware that our comparable-title model could *make* the categories it claims to find? Galbraith would recommend a structural redesign: clear roles for analytics leadership, real-time decision processes for advising clients, a Star Model alignment of strategy, structure, processes, rewards, and people, and possibly a new business unit selling Publikum's analytics layer to producers and distributors. The CEO who took all four seriously would probably end up with a more honest, more reflexive, and slightly slower-moving company than one who only read Galbraith. But a more sustainable and more credible one too.
