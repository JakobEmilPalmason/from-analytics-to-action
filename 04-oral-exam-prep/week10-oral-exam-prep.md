# Week 10 — Data's Environmental Impact: Oral Exam Prep

## 1. Week overview

This week closes Theme 3 ("Critical Perspectives on Data") by forcing the abstraction "data" back into matter — the minerals, water, electricity, racks, and e-waste that make computation physically possible — and asking what gets harmed, and who resists, when AI's infrastructure is actually built. Valdivia's (2024) ethnographic study of the Querétaro data-centre hub is the central object: she imports Anna Tsing's (2009) concept of *supply chain capitalism* and uses it to argue that critical AI studies has so far over-focused on output harms (bias, opacity, surveillance) and ignored the *infrastructural* harms — water dispossession, mineral extractivism, e-waste — that are produced upstream and downstream of the algorithm itself. Brit Ross Winthereik's guest lecture brings the same lens home, showing the Danish hyperscaler boom (Apple/Viborg, Meta/Odense, Microsoft) as another node in the same chain, with the same gap between promised jobs/innovation and delivered water-stress, grid pressure, and citizen exclusion. The practical pay-off is a concept of *algorithmic harm* extended through the full supply chain, and a vocabulary for asking — as a future data-driven manager — *what was extracted and from whom for the model I am about to deploy?*

## 2. Key concepts

- **Supply chain capitalism** — Tsing's (2009) concept: "commodity chains based on subcontracting, outsourcing, and allied arrangements in which the autonomy of component enterprises is legally established even as the enterprise are disciplined within the chain as a whole" (Tsing, 2009, p. 148; cited in Valdivia, 2024, p. 5).
- **Supply chain capitalism of AI** — Valdivia's extension: "the orchestration of commodity chains that extract, ship, and manufacture the natural resources needed to develop AI from an infrastructural perspective, such as mines, data centres and e-waste dumps together with their human resources" (Valdivia, 2024, p. 5).
- **Algorithmic harms (extended)** — Valdivia argues critical AI studies has analysed the *political* impact of algorithms but "infrastructural harms driven by supply chains of AI have been overlooked" (Valdivia, 2024, p. 2); harms must include water dispossession, mining damage, e-waste contamination, labour exploitation across the chain.
- **Algorithmic resistance** — building on Velkova & Kaun (2021): contestation of algorithmic systems; Valdivia widens it to community resistance against data centres themselves (Maconí, "Tu Nube Seca Mi Río"; Valdivia, 2024, p. 4, 11).
- **Commodification of nature** — the conversion of water, minerals, land into priced inputs of capitalist production; in the Querétaro case "drinking water — which is used as a commodification of nature — for their economic activities" (Valdivia, 2024, p. 1).
- **Critical infrastructure perspective** — analytic move that treats data-centres, GPUs, cables, mines, and e-waste dumps as constitutive of AI rather than incidental to it (Valdivia, 2024, p. 1; Brodie & Velkova, 2021).
- **Hyperscale data centre** — Winthereik's working definition (lecture, slide 10): 5,000+ servers, ≥10,000 sq ft (≈1 ha), ≥40 MW capacity; designed for *elastic computing* and *data mirroring* across multiple sites (slide 11).
- **Fabless manufacturing** — NVIDIA's strategy: design but outsource fabrication ("employ key suppliers for all phases of the manufacturing process, including wafer fabrication, assembly, testing, and packing", NVIDIA, 2022, p. 4; cited Valdivia, 2024, p. 6); the legal device that makes responsibility hard to assign (lecture, slide 28).
- **E-waste as the closing stage of the AI chain** — "GPUs have a lifespan of 3 to 5 years… this last stage of the supply chain capitalism of AI illustrates the importance of connecting data centres with landfills and recycling plants, which are often overlooked as the end point of supply chains" (Valdivia, 2024, p. 8).
- **Class niches in supply chains** — Tsing (2009): "supply chains draw upon and vitalise class niches" (Tsing, 2009, p. 158; in Valdivia, 2024, p. 8); operationalised by Valdivia's wage comparison: miner in Brazil ≈ 5 €/hr, data labeller in Kenya < 2 €/hr, OpenAI engineer ≈ 75 €/hr (Valdivia, 2024, p. 8).
- **Geographical asymmetry** — supply chains "proliferat[e] difference within structures of economic power, grow[ing] the gap between rich and poor and genera[ting] varied forms of hierarchy and exclusion" (Neilson & Rossiter, 2021, p. 107; in Valdivia, 2024, p. 3).
- **Sociotechnical lens** — Winthereik (lecture, slide 27): data-centre issues are "composed by social, technical and political elements"; engineering work is "embedded in global power relations".
- **Energy gentrification** — Libertson, Velkova & Palm (2021), cited Valdivia (2024, p. 3): the displacement of local energy/water access by industrial users with stronger claims; Maconí and the Danish hearings are two empirical instances.
- **Boundary opacity of the chain** — Valdivia, working in Querétaro: "it was impossible to know how much water the data centres industry is using in Querétaro" (Valdivia, 2024, p. 11); a chain whose effects are concentrated locally but whose accountability is dispersed globally.

## 3. Per-paper notes

### Valdivia, A. (2024), "The supply chain capitalism of AI: a call to (re)think algorithmic harms and resistance through environmental lens," *Information, Communication & Society*

**(a) Core argument.** Critical AI studies has under-theorised the *infrastructural* dimension of AI. Re-reading AI through Tsing's (2009) "supply chain capitalism" reveals an entire production line — mines (silicon, copper, tantalum, tungsten), fabless chip design (NVIDIA) and outsourced manufacture (TSMC), data centres, and e-waste dumps — that "creates both great wealth and great poverty" (Tsing, 2009, p. 171; in Valdivia, p. 2). The Querétaro fieldwork makes this concrete: the Mexican data-centre hub commodifies water from an Indigenous community (Maconí) suffering severe drought, in order to cool servers used in part to train ChatGPT. The contribution is therefore both theoretical (extend the supply-chain frame to AI) and empirical (show that "algorithmic harms" must include water dispossession, mineral extractivism, and e-waste, not only bias and opacity).

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Three months of fieldwork in México (October 2023 onwards), centred on Querétaro. "16 semi-structured interviews to private actors related with the supply chain of AI, environmental activists and citizens within México" recruited via snowball sampling (note 17, p. 13). Includes participant observation at the Maconí protest at the Querétaro municipal council. Documentary corpus: NVIDIA's *Conflict Minerals Report* (2022), Euromines reports, Microsoft press releases, the LX Legislatura de Querétaro's gazette, the Bajo Tierra Museo del Agua report (2023), local press coverage. Theoretical anchor: Tsing (2009), with substantial engagement with Dyer-Witheford (2015), Taffel (2019), Crawford (2021), Hristova/Neilson/Rossiter (2022), Brodie (2020, 2023), Hogan (2015), Monserrate (2022).

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- "Artificial Intelligence (AI) is woven into a supply chain of capital, commodities and human labour that has been neglected in critical debates" (Abstract, p. 1).
- "The literature on algorithmic harms… and resistance… has largely analysed the political impact of algorithmic systems. However, infrastructural harms driven by supply chains of AI have been overlooked" (p. 2).
- "The development of advanced AI products by industrial and economic elites relies on a supply chain capitalism that 'creates both great wealth and great poverty'" (citing Tsing, 2009, p. 171; Valdivia, p. 2).
- On Tsing's invitation: "stay 'politically close to supply chain hierarchies' and shed light on how exploitation becomes possible through classism, sexism and racism" (citing Tsing, 2009, p. 172; Valdivia, p. 5).
- Wage comparison illustrating class niches: "while a miner in Brazil earns 5 EUR per hour, a data labellers earns less than 2 EUR per hour and an AI engineer at OpenAI makes 75 EUR per hour" (p. 8).
- On e-waste as the chain's terminus: "GPUs have a lifespan of 3 to 5 years. This last stage of the supply chain capitalism of AI illustrates the importance of connecting data centres with landfills and recycling plants, which are often overlooked as the end point of supply chains" (p. 8).
- Querétaro's water politics: industry water consumption "grown from 4% in 2003 to 40% in 2018"; industrial parks "have grown 17 times between 1970 and 2017" by "advancing over areas of ecological value and aquifer recharge zones, degrading environmental systems and compromising water sustainability" (citing Bajo Tierra Museo del Agua, 2023, p. 19; Valdivia, p. 11).
- On opacity: "it was impossible to know how much water the data centres industry is using in Querétaro" (p. 11).
- Field testimony from Juan, Maconí: "We walk for the water we need… in total, it's a 4 hours journey each day to fetch water" (p. 1-2).
- Closing image: build "a world of many worlds where everybody fits" (citing Blaser, 2018; Valdivia, p. 12).

**(d) Connection to the week.** Valdivia is the week's empirical anchor. She gives the conceptual move (extend "algorithmic harm" through the supply chain), the empirical case (Querétaro / Maconí), and the political stakes (water dispossession from Indigenous communities to cool ChatGPT-serving racks). The reading questions in the week description map directly onto her argument: *what are environmental algorithmic harms?* (the mining-to-e-waste chain); *how does supply chain capitalism illuminate global/colonial dynamics?* (Latin America "as a supplier of raw materials… environmental injustice and power inequality", Romero, 2021, in Valdivia, p. 1); *what would a managerial response look like?* (the gap she names between algorithmic-fairness concerns and the chain itself).

## 4. Lecture connections

Brit Ross Winthereik (TME, DTU) brings Valdivia's frame into Northern Europe, with three learning objectives stated up front (slide 2): understand data as a *material* system; identify environmental impacts; apply *supply chain capitalism* and *algorithmic harm*; reflect on responsibility. Her opening question — "Where is your data right now? Why would you bother?" (slide 3) — is the methodological hinge of the lecture: the rest of the slides locate "your data" in physical sites — Viborg, Odense, Eastern/Western Denmark — and argue that bothering is the *first* step of any sociotechnical analysis.

The empirical core (slides 9, 17-18, 20) draws on her own fieldwork: Apple in Viborg (2017–2020), Meta in Odense (2019–2023), Microsoft in Eastern and Western Denmark (2024– ), with the Apple Viborg case (slide 20) used to puncture the industry's job-creation promise: "300 jobs during construction… Apple initially planned 50 operational staff, with a long-term plan of up to 150… By 2025: 8 employees at the data center". The hyperscaler footprint in Denmark — 70 operational and 12 in process (slide 18) — is then read against the Danish renewable-energy mandate (≥55% renewable supply): if AI demand grows faster than the renewable build-out, "it will threaten society's green transition" (slide 21).

Slides 13 and 23-25 inventory the local environmental costs in a register that closely mirrors Valdivia's: water (cooling using *drinking* water, in a country where the supply is already pressured by PFAS and agricultural pollution), energy (grid pressure), air (back-up diesel), noise (humming servers), light (security lighting), and excess heat (which can in principle feed district heating but requires public investment and is not guaranteed). Slide 26 shifts register from infrastructure to citizen affect, quoting Danish hearing responses verbatim: *"meget røvrendte"* (feeling screwed over), *"tromlet over"* (steamrolled), Microsoft *"ligeglade med os"* (doesn't care about us), *"livsværk ødelægges"* (life's work destroyed). This is the Danish counterpart to Maconí's "Water for the people, not for the companies" (Valdivia, p. 10) — different language, same political grammar.

The Quiz scaffolding then locks in the conceptual takeaways: supply chain capitalism is *not* "capitalism is the same everywhere" but "global capitalism is made up of very different labour, laws, and conditions" (slide 6); algorithmic harm is "when the use of algorithms cause harm to individuals, communities and environments" — i.e. distributed across the chain, not only the model output (slides 15-16); responsibility is hard to assign because "firms outsource production to legally distant suppliers" (slide 28). Slide 27's sociotechnical reminder names the four design points where a future data-driven manager could intervene: *architecture*, *infrastructure*, *efficiency*, and a deliberately questioned *data use???* The closing reflection — "How would you design differently?" (slide 31) — is the bridge to the week's third reading question: what would a *managerial* response look like?

## 5. Synthesis with lecture and theme

- **Same chain, different node.** Valdivia and Winthereik are doing the same analysis at two ends of the same chain. Valdivia stands at a Querétaro data-centre hub fed by water dispossessed from Maconí; Winthereik stands at hearings in Jutland over Apple, Meta and Microsoft hyperscalers fed by Danish drinking water and Danish grid capacity. The geographies and severities differ — Maconí walks 4–8 hours for water; Danish farmers fear *"livsværk ødelægges"* — but the analytic claim is identical: the harms of AI cannot be read off the model; they are distributed across the chain that builds and feeds it.
- **From "raw data is an oxymoron" to "raw compute is an oxymoron."** Theme 2 (Week 4) taught that data is *made*, never found. Week 10 extends the same move to *compute*: GPUs are made of silicon, copper, tantalum, tungsten extracted from "Indigenous and peasant lands" with 62% of mine projects in "lands with high drought risks" (Owen et al., 2023, in Valdivia, p. 4). Datafication and computation share a politics: both rely on abstractions ("data", "the cloud") that obscure the people, places, and resources that make them possible. Winthereik's slide 4 — "Data is always produced, processed, and placed somewhere" — is the same point in different vocabulary.
- **Supply chain capitalism re-frames "algorithmic harm".** Up to this week, "algorithmic harm" in critical AI studies has meant bias, opacity, automated discrimination — output-side harms. Valdivia's intervention is to argue that the same systems also produce *infrastructural* harms (water, minerals, e-waste, labour exploitation) that have not been conceptualised as algorithmic at all. Winthereik makes this teachable: "Algorithmic harm must be considered across data's supply chain" (slide 16). For exam purposes this is the single most important reframe of the week.
- **Geographical asymmetry vs. democratic asymmetry.** Valdivia's case foregrounds *colonial* asymmetry — Latin America as historical "supplier of raw materials" (Romero, 2021, p. 414, in Valdivia, p. 1). Winthereik's case foregrounds *democratic* asymmetry within a wealthy, highly digitalised country: citizens "have so far not been provided with much information"; placement is treated as "a 'technical issue' that removes sustainability and other issues away from public scrutiny" (slide 25). The two cases together rebut the easy reading that environmental harms of AI are only a Global South problem.
- **Responsibility is a feature, not a bug, of the chain.** Tsing's original definition stresses that subcontracting "legally established" the autonomy of component enterprises (Tsing, 2009, p. 148, in Valdivia, p. 5). NVIDIA's fabless model (Valdivia, p. 6) is the canonical instance: design in California, fabricate in Taiwan, mine in Brazil/Kazakhstan/Colombia, deploy in Querétaro, dispose in the Global South. Each link can disclaim the others. Winthereik's quiz 3 — "firms outsource production to legally distant suppliers" — is the explicit lesson. For a future data-driven manager, this means responsibility cannot be discharged at any single contractual interface; it has to be reasoned across the whole chain.
- **Resistance has a vocabulary.** Velkova & Kaun (2021) on "algorithmic resistance" and Heemsbergen et al. (2022) on "algorithmic antagonisms" sit behind both Valdivia and the lecture. Maconí ("Water is not a business"; Valdivia, p. 10), *Tu Nube Seca Mi Río* in Spain (Valdivia, p. 4), and the Danish hearings (slide 26) are three flavours of the same posture: communities downstream of compute pushing back on the framing of placement as a "technical issue". This is the anchor for a managerial answer to the week's third reading prompt — listening to that resistance is itself information about where the chain is brittle.

## 6. Likely exam questions

**Q1. What does Valdivia mean by "the supply chain capitalism of AI", and why is the *capitalism* word doing work?**
Valdivia takes Tsing's (2009) concept — "commodity chains based on subcontracting, outsourcing, and allied arrangements in which the autonomy of component enterprises is legally established even as the enterprise are disciplined within the chain as a whole" (Tsing, 2009, p. 148; Valdivia, 2024, p. 5) — and applies it to the production line that makes AI possible: mines, fabless chip design (NVIDIA), outsourced fabrication (TSMC), data centres, and e-waste dumps (Valdivia, p. 5-8). The *capitalism* word matters because the alternative reading — "AI has a supply chain" — would read as a logistics fact. Tsing's frame insists on three further claims: that the chain *vitalises class niches* (Tsing, 2009, p. 158; Valdivia, p. 8), that it deliberately disperses operations to "optimise labour costs, access to raw materials, or proximity to markets" (Dyer-Witheford, 2015, p. 83; Valdivia, p. 3), and that it "creates both great wealth and great poverty" (Tsing, 2009, p. 171; Valdivia, p. 2). The Querétaro/Maconí case is meant to instantiate all three at once.

**Q2. What does Valdivia mean by "infrastructural harms" and how does this re-shape the algorithmic-harms literature?**
The standard algorithmic-harms literature (Diberardino et al., 2024; Velkova & Kaun, 2021) reads harm at the output: bias, opacity, discriminatory automation. Valdivia argues that this leaves the *infrastructural* layer un-analysed: "infrastructural harms driven by supply chains of AI have been overlooked" (p. 2). Concretely, that means harms produced by mineral extraction (Owen et al., 2023, on mines on Indigenous and peasant lands; Valdivia, p. 4), by water and energy consumption at data centres (Hogan, 2015; Monserrate, 2022; Valdivia, p. 4), by labour conditions at data-labelling firms (Miceli & Posada, 2022; Valdivia, p. 8), and by e-waste contamination (Akese & Little, 2018; Valdivia, p. 4, 8). The reshaping is methodological as much as moral: a complete ethical assessment of an AI system has to follow the chain, not stop at the model. Winthereik's slide 16 puts it crisply: "Algorithmic harm must be considered across data's supply chain."

**Q3. Walk through the Querétaro / Maconí case and state what it is meant to show.**
Querétaro hosts 10 operational data centres with plans for 18 more (Valdivia, p. 1-2, 9), attracted by industrial-cluster legacy (US automotive in the 2000s, then aerospace), geography (two hours from Mexico City, sea-cable landings) and a co-operative local government (which "yielded previously public land to an international data centre company", Valdivia, p. 9). Microsoft alone plans three centres consuming an estimated 49.50 MW for ChatGPT and other services (Valdivia, p. 9). 150 km away, the Indigenous and rural community of Maconí walks four to eight hours daily for water because Acueducto II — built after a 2006 agreement promising Maconí improved infrastructure and drinking water — pumps "almost 50M m³" annually from their springs to Querétaro's industrial parks (Valdivia, p. 10-11). The 2023 protest's slogans — "It's not drought, it's looting", "Water is not a business" (p. 10) — name the politics. The case is meant to show that ChatGPT's water footprint is not an abstract "secret water footprint" (Li et al., 2023): it is dispossession from a specific, named community, and it is what Tsing's frame predicts.

**Q4. Why does Valdivia spend a section on labour figures (3.4) and what would the standard "AI-and-labour" debate miss without it?**
Standard debates focus on either (i) AI-engineer wages and white-collar disruption or (ii) data-labelling sweatshops (Perrigo, 2023; Miceli & Posada, 2022). Valdivia's section 3.4 holds *all* the labour figures of the chain in the same frame: a tungsten miner in Brazil, a TSMC fab worker in Taiwan, a data-centre engineer in Querétaro, a data labeller in Kenya, an AI engineer in the UK. Hourly earnings — "miner in Brazil earns 5 EUR… data labellers earns less than 2 EUR… AI engineer at OpenAI makes 75 EUR" (Valdivia, p. 8) — operationalise Tsing's claim that supply chains "draw upon and vitalise class niches" (Tsing, 2009, p. 158; Valdivia, p. 8). Without this section, the "AI labour" question collapses into either coding-job futures or content-moderation ethics; *with* it, the question becomes one of co-ordinated hierarchy across continents, and the analytic move that says "the labeller is the same kind of figure as the miner" is now defensible.

**Q5. Connect Winthereik's Danish material to Valdivia. Same lens or different problem?**
Same lens, different problem. Winthereik's Apple-Viborg case (lecture, slide 20) repeats Valdivia's claim about industry promises: 300 construction jobs, 80 operational planned, 50 then announced, 150 long-term — *eight* operational employees by 2025. The 70 hyperscalers operational in Denmark with 12 more in the pipeline (slide 18) put the same pressure on Denmark's drinking-water supply (already PFAS-stressed, slide 23) and renewable-energy mandate (slide 21) that Querétaro's hub puts on Maconí. The differences are (a) severity — Danish farmers face displacement, not four-hour walks for water — and (b) register of resistance: Maconí marches in the streets ("Water for the people"), Danish citizens give hearing testimony in registers of "anxiety, frustration, powerlessness, indignation, sadness, loss, fear" (slide 26). Both cases refute the framing of data-centre placement as a purely "technical issue" (slide 25) and both rebut the easy claim that environmental harms of AI are a Global South problem.

**Q6. The week's reading questions ask: how would you respond to algorithmic harms as a data-driven manager? Sketch a defensible answer.**
Three layers, ordered from most to least defensible. (1) *Procurement and disclosure*: refuse to deploy on infrastructure whose water/energy/origin you cannot characterise; require chain-of-custody disclosure from cloud providers, mirroring NVIDIA's *Conflict Minerals Report* obligations (Valdivia, p. 6). (2) *Architectural restraint*: read Winthereik's slide 27 — *architecture, infrastructure, efficiency, data use???* — as a checklist; ask whether the analytics question requires generative AI at all, whether on-device inference would do, whether retraining cadence can be reduced. The week's "data use???" deliberately questioned: not every problem is an AI problem, and choosing the smaller model is a supply-chain decision. (3) *Listening to resistance as information*: communities downstream — Maconí, *Tu Nube Seca Mi Río*, the Jutland hearings — are signal, not noise; their objections name the chain's brittleness and should feed back into vendor selection. Acknowledge limits: a single manager cannot rebuild NVIDIA's fabless model; what they can do is refuse to treat the chain as outside their decision boundary. Valdivia would likely insist that this remains palliative; structural change requires the regulatory and political moves she gestures towards in section 5 (Valdivia, p. 11-12).

**Q7. What is the role of "fabless manufacturing" in the argument, and why does Valdivia name NVIDIA so much?**
NVIDIA is the keystone case because its business model makes the chain visible: it designs GPUs but "utilise[s] a fabless manufacturing strategy, whereby [they] employ key suppliers for all phases of the manufacturing process" (NVIDIA, 2022, p. 4; Valdivia, p. 6). Manufacture is outsourced primarily to TSMC (a 1998-vintage partnership; Valdivia, p. 6); minerals are sourced from "259 companies across Asia, Africa, North America, South America, Europe, and Australia" (NVIDIA, 2022; Valdivia, p. 6). The fabless model is not incidental: it is a legal and contractual technology for distributing risk and responsibility across the chain — exactly the dynamic Winthereik's quiz 3 names ("firms outsource production to legally distant suppliers", slide 28). It is also why Microsoft's recent move to design its own *Maia 100* AI accelerator and rack architecture (Valdivia, p. 7) is significant: it both shortens the chain *and* extends supply-chain capitalism into hyperscalers' core operations.

**Q8. What does Valdivia mean by "frictions within the supply chain capitalism of AI" (Section 5)?**
"Frictions" names the contradictions that surface when the chain meets local communities. In Querétaro the friction is between the local government's promise that data centres deliver "innovation and employment" and the observable fact that "while the data centre industry is making huge profit in Querétaro, the rural and Indigenous community of Maconí does not have access to water" (Valdivia, p. 11). Frictions are analytically productive: they make the chain *visible* (otherwise opaque — Valdivia could not even establish total water consumption, p. 11) and they give resistance somewhere to land (the Maconí march, *Tu Nube Seca Mi Río*, the Danish hearings). Theoretically, friction is what stops the chain from being a smooth flow of value: it is where the political economy of AI gets re-politicised. The conceptual debt is to Tsing's broader anthropology of capitalism, where friction is the texture of global connection.

**Q9. Both Valdivia (p. 13, 'techno-feudal' note) and Morozov (2022) refuse the "techno-feudalism" framing. Why does this matter for the week's argument?**
Some critics argue we have moved beyond capitalism into "techno-feudalism" (rentier extraction by Big Tech platforms; Morozov critiques this in *NLR* 133). Valdivia explicitly sides with Morozov: "rather than engaging with techno-feudalism narratives, these supply chains expose that 'capitalism is moving in the same direction it always [has] been'" (Morozov, 2022, p. 126; in Valdivia, p. 12). The stakes are not nominal: if AI is feudal, the analytic and political response is anti-monopoly; if AI is the *latest stage of capitalism*, the response is the longer anti-capitalist toolkit (labour organising, environmental justice, regulation of extractive industries, decolonial critique). Valdivia's whole frame — Tsing's *supply chain capitalism* — only makes sense under the latter reading. For exam purposes the point is that Week 10 is a continuation of Theme 3's political-economy register (Zuboff's surveillance capitalism, Couldry & Mejias's data colonialism), not a break with it.

**Q10. What is the methodological pay-off of Valdivia's three-month fieldwork in Querétaro?**
Two pay-offs. First, *visibility*: only embedded fieldwork makes the AQII pipeline visible as a 123 km route from Maconí springs to Querétaro industrial parks (Valdivia, p. 10), because the documentary record (Conagua reports, the LX Legislatura gazette) reads them separately. Second, *opacity in the right places*: Valdivia tries and fails to learn how much water the data-centre industry uses, and the failure is itself her finding — "it was impossible to know how much water the data centres industry is using in Querétaro" (p. 11). The point recurs in Winthereik's lecture: hyperscaler placement in Denmark is treated as "a technical issue" that "removes sustainability and other issues away from public scrutiny" (slide 25). Methodologically, both researchers turn this opacity into an object of analysis — the chain produces unknowability as a feature — rather than a research limitation.

## 7. One-minute elevator answer

Week 10 is about what gets left out when we talk about "AI": minerals, water, electricity, racks, e-waste, and the people in mines and data-labelling rooms whose labour makes the supply chain run. Valdivia's reading takes Anna Tsing's concept of *supply chain capitalism* and applies it to AI as an entire production line, from silicon and tantalum mines through NVIDIA's fabless GPU design and TSMC's fabs to data centres in Querétaro and e-waste dumps in the Global South. Her fieldwork shows that ChatGPT-serving data centres in Querétaro use water dispossessed from the Indigenous community of Maconí, who walk four to eight hours daily for water. Brit Ross Winthereik brings the same lens to Denmark — Apple in Viborg promised 80 jobs and delivered eight, while hyperscalers consume drinking water and pressure the renewable-energy grid. The take-away is that "algorithmic harm" cannot stop at the model; for a future data-driven manager, it has to follow the whole chain.
