# Week 6 — Participatory Data Design: Oral Exam Prep

## 1. Week overview

This week closes Theme 2 ("Making Data Valuable") by turning from the *concept* of datafication (Week 4) and the *exploratory* mode of analysis (Week 5) to the question of *whom data work is done with and for*. Participatory Data Design (PDD) names a way of involving stakeholders upstream — in datafication, analysis, and presentation — so that the choices about how the world is rendered into data, what counts as a relevant pattern, and what the analysis is *for* can be opened to negotiation rather than fixed in the engine room. Jensen, Birkbak, Madsen and Munk (2021) supply the empirical practice — data sprints, three key activities (datafication, flexible visualization, materialization), and a working concept of *data as a boundary object* between researchers and stakeholders. Madsen (2024) supplies the philosophical scaffolding: digital methods place analysts in *vague empirical situations* in which the link between measure and value has to be re-built, and the act of operationalizing concepts becomes the engine of intersubjective sensemaking — what he calls *experimental a priori*. The practical pay-off is a working answer to the lecturer's prompt: how do you ensure your project aligns with the case company's interests, generates real hypotheses rather than confirming preconceptions, and is not simply absorbed into their existing categories?

## 2. Key concepts

- **Participatory Data Design (PDD)** — the involvement of stakeholders in data science projects through "active participation, typically upstream, influencing choices relating to dafication, analysis, and/or presentation" (lecture, slide 5; Jensen et al., 2021, p. 117).
- **Data sprints** — "three- to five-day intensive workshops where STS researchers collaborate with stakeholders in a particular field to collect and generate data that is relevant to a particular problem—and together create a useful digital device or object" (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 117).
- **Data as boundary object** — Jensen et al. extend Star & Griesemer's notion: data and visualizations function as shared objects "the stakeholders can immediately relate to their knowledge of the field" (2021, p. 128), forcing different communities to discuss with reference to the same artefact.
- **Datafication (PDD sense)** — "the process of gathering a substantial amount of digital data and relating it to a particular problem" (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 123); the authors explicitly use it as a *neutral* word, against Couldry/Mejias's critical valence (note 5, p. 132).
- **Flexible visualization** — quickly producible, modifiable visualizations (especially network maps in Gephi) that play "the same work that cheap, pliable materials such as cardboard boxes and Lego brick models did in the Scandinavian tradition of participatory design" (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 127).
- **Materialization** — the third PDD practice: producing tangible outcomes (prototypes, interfaces, leaflets) that "become a device for articulating the participating stakeholders' knowledge" (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 129).
- **The John Score** — emergent device from the Danish Board of Technology / Zika project: after algorithmic filtering, John (the citizen-engagement expert) reads through the remaining tweets and rates their relevance for the leaflet (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 119; lecture slide 11).
- **Slowing down reasoning by speeding up data** — Jensen et al.'s slogan for PDD's deconstructive effect: large datasets and the need to operationalize concepts "challenge quick assumptions" and force suspended judgement (2021, p. 131).
- **Constitutionalism vs. interference** — Law's (2009) distinction used by Jensen et al. (p. 129): is the goal to keep the situation messy enough to resist a single ontology (interference), or to craft a common world from multiple positions (constitutionalism)? It varies project by project.
- **Vague empirical situation** — Dewey (1938) via Madsen (2024, pp. 96-97): a situation "in which existing concepts and classificatory schemes lose their heuristic value as guides of thought and action", forcing reconstructive work that Dewey takes to be "the core of critical thinking".
- **Operationalization (Peircean)** — Madsen (2024, pp. 99-100) reads Peirce: "the meaning of concepts must lie in their tangible effects"; deciding *which operations realize a concept* is the only way for an "intersubjective community of enquirers" to discuss it.
- **Pragmatic a priori** — C. I. Lewis (1923) via Madsen (2024, p. 104): structures of thought that "represent an attitude in some sense freely taken, a stipulation of the mind itself, and a stipulation which might have been in some other way if it suited our bent and need" — chosen because they fit the problem, not because the world compels them.
- **Experimental a priori** — Madsen's contribution: digital methods make Lewis's pragmatic a priori "permanently beta" (2024, p. 106), letting analysts iteratively *switch between* structuring principles (e.g. dot-map vs. heatmap vs. network) during the analysis itself.
- **Alien data source** — the first design principle: deliberately seek data that "trouble your native categorical schemes" (Madsen, 2024, p. 108) so that established links between measure and value break and recalibration becomes necessary.
- **Sense of dissonance** — Stark (2011) via Madsen (2024, p. 108): the productive friction between competing evaluative principles that an alien data source produces and that keeps analysts attentive.
- **Docile data situation** — Munk et al. (2019) via Madsen (2024, p. 106): the failure mode of digital methods in which the underdeterminacy of data is used "to legitimise almost any pre-existing individual agenda or narrative" (the Israel's Square workshop is the cautionary case).
- **Diagrammatic reasoning** — Peirce (1906) via Madsen (2024, pp. 105-106): formal schemata (maps, networks, equations) function as scaffolds within which thought can be guided through "rule-bound experiments, the consequences of which can then be discussed".
- **Backcasting** — the fifth design step (Madsen, 2024, p. 110): after abductive sensemaking, return to the operationalizations and ask *which assumptions made these stories possible, and should they be revised?*
- **Issue publics / controversies** — Marres (2005) and Latour (2004) via Jensen et al. (2021, pp. 120-121): the conceptual frame within which digital methods were developed in STS — publics form *around issues*, not the other way round.

## 3. Per-paper notes

### Jensen, T. E., Birkbak, A., Madsen, A. K. & Munk, A. K. (2021), "Participatory Data Design: Acting in a Digital World"

**(a) Core argument.** PDD is an STS *making and doing* practice — developed at the Techno-Anthropology Lab (TANTlab) since 2015 — in which stakeholders are brought into data work upstream, around shared visualizations, in workshop formats called data sprints. It is not a finished framework but "a demo in the shape of a number of stories" (p. 117) about three recurring practices — *datafication*, *flexible visualization*, *materialization* — and the dilemmas they generate. The central conceptual move is to use *data as a boundary object* between STS researchers and external stakeholders: the visualizations are simultaneously objects of persuasion (drawing things together, in Latour's sense) and objects of negotiation (low-fidelity prototypes, in the participatory-design sense).

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Demo chapter, no formal empirics. Draws on five PDD projects 2015-2018: the Danish Board of Technology / Zika citizen hearing (the opening "John" story), obesity research with Oxford, the Royal Danish Theatre / authenticity project, the Aalborg school-reform project, and a fake-news project. Mobilizes STS, the Scandinavian participatory design tradition (Ehn & Kyng 1992; Kensing & Blomberg 1998), and digital methods (Rogers 2013; Marres 2015).

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- On the John Score: "the workshop participants invented what they half-jokingly called the John score. Leaning on John's expertise, they asked John to read through the tweets and rate their relevance for the information leaflet" (p. 119).
- On flexible visualization as Lego: "visualization can do some of the same work that cheap, pliable materials such as cardboard boxes and Lego brick models did in the Scandinavian tradition of participatory design" (p. 127).
- On the deconstructive effect: "the aim should be to slow down reasoning by speeding up data. Quick assumptions about, say, the unity of the field of obesity research or the typical views of parents on school reform may be challenged if one can collect and explore a large data set" (p. 131).
- On data as boundary object: "When participants have collectively agreed on an operationalization (of, e.g., authenticity) they are likely to take an interest in and try to relate to the visual outcome of that operationalization" (p. 126).
- On loss of control: "we as PDD researchers became very attractive collaborators for those already in a powerful position, something that empowers and betrays at the same time, because we appreciated the attention but did not necessarily agree with being mobilized as evidence against the teachers" (p. 131, on the Aalborg school-reform case).
- On the role of materialization: "anything we produce in a PDD workshop is at the mercy of many indirectly involved stakeholders and obdurate organizational practices" (p. 130, on the citizen hearing where the tweets were "scarcely mentioned in the discussions").

**(d) Connection to the week.** This is the week's *practical* reading: it gives the vocabulary (datafication, flexible visualization, materialization), the workshop format (data sprint), the canonical anecdote (the John Score), and the named *risks* (loss of control, fact-quality of maps, becoming "merchants of multiplicity", p. 132). It is the reading the lecturer's case-work prompts (slide 30) hang directly on: alignment with stakeholder interests, vetting of new hypotheses, resistance to imposed interpretation.

### Madsen, A. K. (2024), "Digital methods as 'experimental a priori' — how to navigate vague empirical situations as an operationalist pragmatist"

**(a) Core argument.** The computational turn places analysts in *vague empirical situations* — data that are "multi-authored, granular, relational and increasingly possible to treat with inductive algorithmic techniques" (p. 111) — in which the inherited links between measurements and values break down. Drawing on Kant, Peirce, Dewey and C. I. Lewis, Madsen argues that this vagueness has a distinctive *critical potential* if it is harnessed deliberately: (i) the act of *operationalization* becomes an engine of intersubjective semantic clarity (Peirce); (ii) the availability of multiple structuring principles (maps vs. heatmaps vs. networks) lets analysts iteratively switch the *a priori* of the analysis itself, which Madsen calls *experimental a priori*. The critical potential is not automatic — left alone, vagueness produces "docile data" — and the paper closes with five design principles (alien source → operationalize → algorithm → abduction → backcasting).

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Conceptual paper grounded in two years of qualitative ethnographic / interview material from the *Do You Live in a Bubble?* (DYLIAB) collaboration between TANTlab and GEHL Architects. The empirical hooks are vignettes with Jeff Risom (CIO) and Alex (innovation associate) puzzling over a Facebook-trace map of urban political diversity, plus a contrast workshop ("Israel's Square") where the design failed and personal preferences took over.

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- On the central pragmatist claim: "the meaning of concepts must lie in their tangible effects. The semantic test of a concept is to explicate which procedures to follow to obtain perceptual acquaintance with the objects falling under it" (p. 99, glossing Peirce 1878).
- On Lewis on the pragmatic a priori: "What is a priori is necessary truth not because it compels the minds acceptance, but precisely because it does not [...] represents an attitude in some sense freely taken, a stipulation of the mind itself" (Lewis 1923, quoted p. 104).
- On the experimental version: "in the context of digital methods, Lewis' pragmatic a priori is permanently beta. The consequence is that established concepts — such as space in the case of Jeff and Alex — are opened for redefinition" (p. 106).
- On the docile failure mode: "Rather than engaging in the hard work of deciding on a shared operationalisation for identifying interesting areas, we imposed our idiosyncratic interests on it. This was nicely captured by one of the GEHL participants, who dryly stated that she thought we should try this method in a city that none of us knew beforehand" (p. 107, the Israel's Square workshop).
- On the Jeff/Alex case as conceptual work: "By stripping the map for accidental qualities such as the green dots, Jeff and Alex prepare the ground for focused data explorations that they find relevant to the problem they are trying to solve. In this rendering, private venues can no longer be agents of diversity" (p. 102).
- On the design imperative: "the critical potential cannot be realised without deliberate design interventions" (p. 112), justifying the five-step process.

**(d) Connection to the week.** This is the week's *philosophical* reading: it tells you *why* PDD's procedures work when they work, and *why* they collapse when they don't. The Jeff-and-Alex vignettes are the worked examples of "having data as a boundary object" — the dotted map is alien enough to GEHL's PSPL toolkit ("the Gehl lens", lecture slide 23) that conceptual distinctions like *place vs. event* or *public vs. private space* must be operationalized rather than presumed. The Israel's Square workshop is the cautionary tale that the lecturer reproduces (slide 8 onward, by contrast): without an explicit procedure, even a well-equipped lab will fall back on its preferred stories.

## 4. Lecture connections

Anders Koed Madsen frames the lecture (slides 4-5) by giving PDD a tight definition that pulls forward directly from Jensen et al.: *involvement* is "active participation, typically upstream, influencing choices relating to dafication, analysis, and/or presentation". Two things matter: *upstream*, because critical decisions otherwise lock in before stakeholders can speak; and *active*, because tacit knowledge of the field will not surface unless stakeholders are forced to react to a concrete artefact. The data-sprint format (slide 5) is presented as the operational vehicle for this involvement.

The lecture is then organized around two purposes of stakeholder involvement — and these map cleanly onto the two readings. **Purpose 1: alignment.** Slides 6-13 work through the Zika / Danish Board of Technology case from Jensen et al. (2021), with the John Score (slide 11) as the punchline. The point is that without John in the room, the cluster maps printed by TANTlab were just hairballs; with John iteratively rating tweet-clusters, the project produced material that could actually enter the Board's leaflet. Slide 12 generalizes: "Allowing stakeholders to participate in the definition of what is interesting. Keeping decisions about datafication and analysis as open as possible. Using intermediary visualizations as concrete occasions for stakeholders to provide feedback." Slide 14 quotes Jensen et al.'s Lego analogy verbatim.

**Purpose 2: challenging assumptions and generating hypotheses.** Slides 20-29 work through the GEHL / DYLIAB case from Madsen (2024). The lecturer juxtaposes "the Gehl Lens" (slide 23: PSPL — observers, surveys, predefined street segments) with "the Facebook lens" (slides 24-25: anonymous user traces, venue dots), and shows the resulting diversity datascape (slides 26-28). Slide 29 — "Outside Gehl's control vs. Within Gehl's control" — is the crucial pedagogic move: the friction between an *alien data source* and the company's existing toolkit forces them to revisit conceptions of public space, public vs. private, and the geographical unit of analysis. This is exactly Madsen's "experimental a priori" rendered as a stakeholder workflow.

The Gephi Lite demo (slides 15-19, the "global market of professional chefs" dataset) sits between the two halves: it is the technical instrument that makes flexible visualization feasible at workshop tempo. Slide 19's instruction to students — "to find patterns that seem interesting" and "to list who you would ideally invite in to help you explore this data" — is itself a PDD prompt: the choice of stakeholders is part of the design.

The closing prompts (slide 30) frame the case-presentation week explicitly around three PDD challenges: (i) alignment ("how will you ensure that your data project is aligned with the interests of your case companies, given that these interests will likely become much clearer... when they see your presentation?"); (ii) hypothesis generation ("how will you ensure that the new questions and hypotheses... are also vetted and prioritized?"); (iii) interpretive autonomy ("how will you ensure that your case companies do not impose the interpretations that align with their preconceived ideas on your results?").

## 5. Cross-paper synthesis

- **Both treat datafication as a problem-shaping practice, not a data-collection task.** Jensen et al. define it as "the process of gathering a substantial amount of digital data and relating it to a particular problem" (p. 123), and use the Royal Danish Theatre / authenticity discussion to show how operationalizing the concept of *authenticity* in a Facebook dataset re-opens a concept the participants thought they knew. Madsen makes the same move philosophically explicit via Peirce: "decisions on operationalisations afford a distinct form of intersubjective sensemaking" (p. 96). The two papers agree that the *value* of digital data work lies in the discussions provoked by trying to render concepts operational.

- **Both name the same failure mode and the same antidote.** Jensen et al. warn that visualizations can become "more persuasive than we might want" (p. 126) and that participants impose pre-existing knowledge "by quickly and directly associating the entities they know from the field with specific clusters" (p. 126). Madsen names this the *docile data situation* (p. 106) and gives it a worked example in the Israel's Square workshop: with no formal procedure, the team voted on areas of personal interest. Both papers locate the antidote in the *materiality* of operationalization — make participants commit to a procedure, then make the procedure visible enough to argue with.

- **Disagreement of register: practice-report vs. philosophical scaffold.** Jensen et al. is a "demo" (p. 117) — stories, dilemmas, situated lessons; it does not theorize *why* the procedures work. Madsen (2024) is unembarrassedly philosophical — Kant, Peirce, Dewey, Lewis — and produces a five-step prescription. The papers complement: Jensen et al. tell you what a PDD project *looks like* on the ground; Madsen tells you what *epistemic work* the boundary object is actually doing and where it can fail.

- **Extension: from "datafication is neutral" to "datafication is epistemically critical."** Jensen et al. explicitly use *datafication* as a "neutral word" (note 5, p. 132), and contrast their position with Couldry/Mejias's critical valence. Madsen's contribution can be read as recovering a *critical* dimension of datafication that is not political-economic but epistemic: the act of operationalizing concepts in alien data sources is itself a critical practice — it forces explication and recalibration. This is a different sense of "critique" than Week 4's surveillance-capitalism / data-colonialism frame, and the two should not be confused.

- **Both reframe the role of the analyst.** Jensen et al. report a "surprising sense of empowerment" combined with a "loss of control" (pp. 130-131): STS researchers become deliverers of persuasive objects, and those objects then travel without them. Madsen's analyst is similarly displaced: she is no longer the stable methodologist of an established toolkit (PSPL) but a designer of recalibration — someone who *deliberately seeks vagueness* (Step 1, p. 108). Both readings end on roughly the same note: the analyst's residual responsibility is procedural — to keep the process intersubjective, the data sets circulating, the operationalizations visible.

## 6. Likely exam questions

**Q1. Define Participatory Data Design and explain its three key practices.**
PDD is the involvement of stakeholders in data science projects, "typically upstream, influencing choices relating to dafication, analysis, and/or presentation" (lecture, slide 5). Jensen et al. (2021) describe three recurring practices: (1) *datafication* — gathering and relating data to a problem, which "stimulates queries... about the normative commitments of the participants" (p. 124), exemplified by the Royal Danish Theatre's discussion of how to operationalize *authenticity* in Facebook traces; (2) *flexible visualization* — quickly producible network maps and other displays that play "the same work that cheap, pliable materials such as cardboard boxes and Lego brick models did in the Scandinavian tradition of participatory design" (p. 127); (3) *materialization* — producing tangible outcomes (a leaflet, an interactive datascape, a prototype) that can "be carried away" (p. 131) by stakeholders into their own organizations. Madsen (2024) supplies the philosophical reason these practices work when they work: the act of operationalizing concepts is "an engine for creating intersubjective clarity about the meaning of existing concepts" (p. 94).

**Q2. What does it mean to treat data as a "boundary object", and why does it matter?**
Boundary objects (Star & Griesemer 1989) are shared artefacts that mediate between communities with different goals. Jensen et al. (2021) extend this: in PDD projects, the visualization functions as a boundary object because once participants "have collectively agreed on an operationalization (of, e.g., authenticity) they are likely to take an interest in and try to relate to the visual outcome of that operationalization" (p. 126). This matters because without a shared object, stakeholders and analysts talk past each other — the John story is the canonical illustration: the printouts of co-hashtag clusters gave John, an expert in citizen engagement with no technical background, a way to bring his intuitions to bear (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 119; lecture slide 11). Madsen (2024) reframes this in pragmatist terms: an artefact built from an explicit operationalization gives "an intersubjective community of enquirers" (p. 99) something tangible to disagree about, which is what allows conceptual distinctions like *place vs. event* (p. 100) to be sharpened rather than left tacit.

**Q3. Explain the John Score. What does it illustrate about PDD?**
At a workshop run jointly by TANTlab and the Danish Board of Technology, John was a citizen-engagement expert overwhelmed by 400,000 Zika-related tweets (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 118). The TANTlab researchers iteratively printed co-hashtag maps, filtered by various criteria, until "the filtering process had returned a limited number of tweets that seemed to cover key aspects of the total material. At this time the workshop participants invented what they half-jokingly called the John score" — John read through the surviving tweets and rated their relevance for the citizen-hearing leaflet (p. 119). It illustrates three things at once: (i) datafication is iterative and stakeholder-shaped, not a one-shot pipeline; (ii) algorithmic filtering and human expertise are complementary, not competitive — the algorithms reduced the corpus to something John could read closely, John's intuition graded what the algorithm could not; (iii) the procedure emerged in the workshop, not in a methods textbook, which is the deeper PDD claim that "PDD projects should avoid the temptation to define the key problems and concepts in advance" (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 124). It also has an unhappy postscript — at the actual citizen hearing the tweets "were scarcely mentioned in the discussions" (p. 130), illustrating that even successful PDD outputs are at the mercy of organizational practice.

**Q4. What is a "vague empirical situation", and how does the GEHL case illustrate it?**
Madsen (2024, pp. 96-97), drawing on Dewey (1938), defines a vague empirical situation as one "in which existing concepts and classificatory schemes lose their heuristic value as guides of thought and action". The GEHL case is the worked example. GEHL's established PSPL toolkit ("the Gehl lens", lecture slide 23) maps urban life through observers stationed in *predefined* public spaces, counting categories like gender. When the firm tries to map urban political diversity using Facebook traces, "in Facebook's native digital ontology, the only geolocated entities are urban venues that own a Facebook page" (p. 100) — *place* and *event* collapse into the same dot. Jeff Risom and Alex therefore have to renegotiate concepts the firm thought it owned: "I want to look at the place — not the event" (p. 100), which has no straightforward translation to the dotted map. The vagueness is productive precisely because it forces the architects to operationalize *space* explicitly — eventually settling on aggregating venue diversity into spatial radii — which Madsen reads as Peircean "pragmatic" sensemaking (p. 99). This is exactly Jensen et al.'s "slow down reasoning by speeding up data" (2021, p. 131) seen from the philosophical side.

**Q5. What does Madsen mean by "experimental a priori", and how does it differ from Kant's and Lewis's versions?**
The *a priori* is what comes before experience and structures it. Kant (1787) treated it as universal and transcendental — Euclidean space, the categories of cause and substance, etc. — fixed for all human cognition (Madsen, 2024, p. 103). C. I. Lewis (1923), writing after Einstein had loosened Newtonian physics, argued that the a priori is *pragmatic*: structures "freely chosen" because "they fit the problem at hand" (Madsen, 2024, p. 104), but still imagined as fixed *before* an investigation. Madsen's contribution is *experimental a priori* (p. 106): in digital methods the a priori is "permanently beta" — analysts can switch between structuring schemes (dotted map vs. heatmap vs. network in the GEHL case, p. 105) on the fly because granular digital data fit multiple structures and exploratory algorithms make iteration cheap. The implication for PDD is methodological: the structuring scheme itself is up for negotiation in the workshop, not a methodological precondition of it. This is what gives PDD its critical edge — analysts and stakeholders are reasoning *over* the a priori, not *from* it.

**Q6. Walk through Madsen's five steps of experimental a priori. Where does the Israel's Square workshop go wrong?**
The five steps are: (1) seek *alien data sources* that destabilize your native categories (Madsen, 2024, p. 108); (2) *operationalize* central concepts collectively, recalibrating the link between measure and value (p. 109); (3) delegate attention to a formal *algorithm* whose outputs become a "stumbling block" against confirmation bias (p. 109); (4) engage in *abductive sensemaking* — qualitative interpretation that uses but does not replace the algorithmic patterns (p. 110); (5) *backcast* by asking which assumptions produced these stories and whether the assumptions should change (p. 110). The Israel's Square workshop fails at Step 2 and therefore at every subsequent step. With no agreed procedure for "interesting place", participants "took turns zooming in on areas that we found relevant or interesting for personal or professional reasons" (p. 107) and ended on a post-it vote — the ironic shorthand "no bias" emerged (p. 107). This is the *docile data situation* (p. 106) Jensen et al. (2021, p. 126) hint at when they warn that participants "impute their previous knowledge of the project's field onto the maps". The lesson is that vagueness is necessary but not sufficient — the design has to make commitment to a procedure unavoidable.

**Q7. How is flexible visualization like a Lego brick, and where does the analogy break down?**
Jensen et al. (2021, p. 127) explicitly invoke Ehn & Kyng's (1992) Lego-brick mock-ups from Scandinavian participatory design: cheap, pliable, modifiable in real time, with "quick feedback between exploring options... and the consequences of that arrangement". Network maps in Gephi or Gephi Lite play this role in PDD because they can be quickly recoloured, refiltered, and reprinted, letting stakeholders intervene without needing to read code (lecture, slide 14). The analogy breaks down on tempo: "in a PDD project the flow of events is often more restrained: An issue expert might suggest a particular way to cut the data... If the group decides that this would be an avenue to pursue, one of us would have to do some work to create the next data visualization. This may be a matter of minutes or hours" (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 128). It also breaks down on literacy: pie charts and line graphs are visually familiar; "the network graph... is challenging for many participants" (p. 126), and "the location of a cluster (up or down, left or right) does not carry any specific meaning" (p. 126). Madsen (2024, p. 109) adds that the genuine instrument here is not the visualization but the *operationalization* behind it — the Lego brick of PDD is the algorithmic procedure made tangible as a map.

**Q8. What does Jensen et al. mean by "slow down reasoning by speeding up data"? Connect to Madsen.**
The phrase appears at Jensen et al. (2021, p. 131) and names PDD's *deconstructive* effect: collecting and exploring large datasets, and being forced to operationalize concepts to do so, "may challenge quick assumptions about, say, the unity of the field of obesity research or the typical views of parents on school reform". The Royal Danish Theatre case is the clearest example — the firm's intuitive concept of *authenticity* fragmented as soon as participants tried to operationalize it in Facebook data (p. 124). Madsen (2024) gives this its philosophical name — Dewey's *suspended judgement* (p. 97), the "form of suspended judgement that the human mind normally prefers to skip". The connection is that the *speeding up* (algorithmic exploration of granular digital data) is what makes the *slowing down* (operationalization, conceptual work, abductive guessing) feasible: large enough data with flexible enough tools so that a community of inquirers cannot skate by on their existing categories. The Israel's Square workshop is the failure case where the speeding-up happened without the slowing-down (Madsen, 2024, p. 107).

**Q9. PDD has "loss of control" and "fact-quality" risks. What are they, and what would you do about them in your own case work?**
Jensen et al. (2021, pp. 130-131) identify three connected risks. *Loss of control*: PDD outputs are "carried away by the stakeholders and incorporated into arguments and decisions that would normally be beyond our reach" — the Aalborg school-reform map was used by politicians "to destabilize the opposition they were facing from the teachers' union" (p. 131). *Fact-quality*: stakeholders begin to describe "the maps as the actual, data-based facts, as opposed to their own previous representations of the field" (p. 130), which both empowers the analyst and accelerates uncontrolled circulation. *Merchants of multiplicity*: PDD researchers can become hired guns producing whichever multiplicity a stakeholder wants visible (p. 132). The remedies are procedural — "stress their constructed nature, the uncertainty of data, and the dangers of reading too much into them" (p. 130-131); "make the data sets produced in PDD projects as widely available as possible" (p. 132); design interactive visualizations "that keep the analysis open for reorientation" (p. 132). For case work the practical version is Madsen's Step 5 (2024, p. 110): present the operationalizations *as choices*, and document what alternative operationalizations would have produced, so the case company has to engage with *which a priori it wants to keep* rather than just receiving a finding.

**Q10. The lecturer's case-presentation prompt asks how to ensure (i) alignment, (ii) hypothesis generation, (iii) interpretive autonomy. Answer it using both readings.**
*Alignment* (Jensen et al., 2021, pp. 123-124): treat the case-company presentation as a datafication moment — invite them to react to a flexible visualization, and use the resulting discussion to operationalize their concerns, much as the Royal Danish Theatre operationalized *authenticity* against a Facebook dataset (p. 124). *Hypothesis generation* (Madsen, 2024, pp. 109-110): adopt Steps 3-4 explicitly — let an exploratory algorithm propose patterns, then ask stakeholders to abduce explanations *especially for surprising patterns*, "in the spirit of Peirce... [working] with areas that they found especially surprising" (p. 110). This is the antidote to the Israel's Square failure mode where preferred stories swallowed the data (p. 107). *Interpretive autonomy* (Jensen et al., 2021, p. 132; Madsen, 2024, p. 110): build in backcasting — present at least two operationalizations side by side, name the design choice each implies, and refuse to deliver a single "fact-quality" map. This is also the lecturer's "Outside Gehl's control / Within Gehl's control" framing (slide 29): visible operationalizations let the company see which features of the analysis are theirs to negotiate and which are imposed by the alien data source.

## 7. One-minute elevator answer

Week 6 is about *participatory data design* — the practice of bringing stakeholders into a data project upstream so they shape the choices about how data is made, structured and read. Jensen, Birkbak, Madsen and Munk give us the working format: the data sprint, with three core practices — datafication, flexible visualization, and materialization — and the John story as the canonical illustration. The visualization is a boundary object: cheap and modifiable like a Lego brick, persuasive enough to anchor discussion. Madsen's *experimental a priori* paper supplies the philosophy: digital methods place us in vague empirical situations, and the act of operationalizing concepts is what creates intersubjective clarity.
