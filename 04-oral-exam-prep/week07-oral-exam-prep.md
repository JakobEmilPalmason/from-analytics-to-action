# Week 7 — Ethical Perspectives on Data-Driven Management: Oral Exam Prep

## 1. Week overview

Week 7 opens Theme 3 ("Challenges in the Data Economy") by shifting the course's question from *how is data made?* (Theme 2) to *what kind of power does data-making produce, and how should that power be governed?* The week's central claim is that data ethics is not a compliance add-on but a way of seeing the **distribution of power** baked into Big Data Socio-Technical Infrastructures (BDSTIs) — and that organisations, citizens and policymakers therefore have to make ethical choices at the level of *infrastructure design and governance*, not only at the level of individual data points. Hasselbalch (2021) supplies the conceptual frame: "Data ethics (of power) addresses the distribution of power and power relations in the Big Data Society and the conditions of their negotiation and distribution" (p. 10, in lecture, slide 11). Micheli et al. (2020) supply a constructive complement: four governance models (Data Sharing Pools, Data Cooperatives, Public Data Trusts, Personal Data Sovereignty) that articulate alternatives to the dominant corporate-platform extraction model. Hasselbalch & Tranberg (2016) — the original "data ethics as competitive advantage" argument — frames why companies should care: ethical data practice is a strategic differentiator, not just a legal floor (lecture, slides 5, 10). The practical pay-off is a *data interest analysis* (slide 14): students should be able to look at any data-driven project — including their case dataset — and answer *whose interests are inscribed in this infrastructure, who is empowered, who is disempowered, and what governance model could redistribute that power?*

## 2. Key concepts

- **Data ethics (of power)** — "addresses the distribution of power and power relations in the Big Data Society and the conditions of their negotiation and distribution" (Hasselbalch, 2021, p. 10; lecture slide 11). Applied data ethics aims to make these power relations visible.
- **Big Data Socio-Technical Infrastructure (BDSTI)** — the spatial, material and immaterial architecture of the Big Data Society; like a road or bridge, it "resides in a 'naturalised background'" until breakdown reveals its politics (Hasselbalch, 2021, p. 14, citing Edwards, 2002).
- **Datafication** — Mayer-Schönberger & Cukier's term, used by Hasselbalch as the engine of the BDSTI: the transformation "of all things (and people) into data formats... in order to 'quantify the world'" (2021, p. 15).
- **Destiny Machines** — Hasselbalch's term for "technological systems and processes designed to predict human behaviour based on the accumulation of personal data and then act on these predictions" (2021, p. 28); they "produce machine-readable people and shell out destinies on the other side of the production line."
- **Surveillance capitalism** — Zuboff's name (cited by Hasselbalch, 2021, p. 29–30) for "an accumulation of a capitalist logic that commodifies human psychology and experience to satisfy market forces" — a structural reframe of the actor of surveillance from state to commercial platform.
- **Liquid surveillance / surveillance-industrial complex** — Lyon's account (in Hasselbalch, 2021, p. 28–29): surveillance "embedded in digital infrastructures, networked, distributed" rather than centralised; sustained by the interrelation of state and private actors (Hayes, 2012).
- **Data double / data derivative** — the abstracted digital representation of a person within a surveillance assemblage; Amoore's twist is that the system is interested "not... on what our data say about us, but on what can be imagined and inferred about who we might be" (Hasselbalch, 2021, p. 28).
- **Asymmetric experience of data power** — the empirical observation that the Big Data Society's risks are not equally distributed: "Surveillance is nothing new to black folks" (Browne, 2015, in Hasselbalch, 2021, p. 32); women, racialised people, migrants and the poor experience data violence directly (p. 33–34).
- **Data ethical governance / spaces of negotiation** — Hasselbalch's normative move: governance must constitute "a critical space of value negotiation characterised by the inclusion of multiple actors" and "an awareness of the very conditions of power between different stakeholders" (2021, p. 36).
- **Voice of data ethics** — Hasselbalch's question "Does Data Ethics Have a Voice?": ethical critique is "not only about power, it is power. The power to raise issues, to define problems, to propose and create the solutions" (2021, p. 34).
- **Data governance (social-science definition)** — Micheli et al. (2020): "the power relations between all the actors affected by, or having an effect on, the way data is accessed, controlled, shared and used, the various socio-technical arrangements set in place to generate value from data, and how such value is redistributed between actors" (p. 3).
- **Data infrastructure (analytical lens)** — drawn from STS/CDS: "an evolving ecosystem with a plurality of actors having multiple interests, agendas, goals and strategies, and interacting with an array of tools, mechanisms, systems, interfaces and devices for governing data" (Micheli et al., 2020, p. 3, citing Kitchin & Lauriault, 2014).
- **Data politics** — Ruppert et al.'s concept (in Micheli et al., 2020, p. 3): data is "an object of investment... produced by the competitive struggles of (actors) who claim stakes in its meaning and functioning"; data is "generative of new forms of power relations."
- **The four governance models (Micheli et al., 2020, p. 6)** — **DSP** (Data Sharing Pools): horizontal partnerships among data holders, "data as commodity"; **DC** (Data Cooperatives): citizens collectively pool and govern data, "data commons"; **PDT** (Public Data Trusts): public bodies act as trustees for "data as public infrastructure"; **PDS** (Personal Data Sovereignty): individuals control access to their own data via personal data spaces, principle of "technological sovereignty."
- **Reciprocity** — Micheli et al.'s fifth analytical dimension (2020, p. 5): the (un)balance of power in accessing, controlling and using data, distinguishing unilateral from mutual governance.
- **Data ethics as competitive advantage** — Hasselbalch & Tranberg's (2016, pp. 9–11) framing, summarised in lecture slide 10: go beyond legal compliance, follow the *spirit* of the law, build a privacy-aware corporate culture, use Privacy by Design, ask "Would I accept this as a consumer? Is this the kind of data environment I want for my children?"
- **Ethics as data innovation** — lecture's reframe (slides 15–16) that each AI HLEG ethical requirement (human agency, robustness, privacy, transparency, fairness, environmental well-being, accountability) "could be a design choice (and a form of innovation)," not a constraint.

## 3. Per-paper notes

### Hasselbalch, G. (2021), "Big Data Socio-Technical Infrastructures (BDSTIs)," ch. 1 of *Data Ethics of Power: A Human Approach in the Big Data and AI Era*

**(a) Core argument.** The Big Data Society is a *spatial* phenomenon: it consists of socio-technical infrastructures that, like Morandi Bridge, "form the silent background against which life and business were facilitated" (p. 14) until a moment of crisis pulls their politics into view. The chapter's task is to make those infrastructures visible and to define **a "data ethics of power"** that addresses the asymmetric, liquid, surveillance-industrial power they produce. Critically, this ethics is not just analytical: it asks *who has the voice* to raise issues and propose solutions, and insists that those subjugated to data power are systematically excluded from the spaces in which solutions are negotiated (pp. 34–37).

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Conceptual chapter from a monograph. Mobilises infrastructure studies (Star, Bowker, Edwards), Lefebvre on the social production of space, Harvey on time–space compression, Castells on the network society's "space of flows," Lyon on surveillance society, Zuboff on surveillance capitalism, and Browne on race and surveillance. Empirical anchors are illustrative: Morandi Bridge (2018), Snowden / PRISM (2013), the EU's Digital Single Market and Cloud strategies, COVID-19 dashboards, the UK A-level grading algorithm (2020), the Dutch SyRi system, EURODAC and ECRIS-TCN, and personal accounts (Emma Holten, Robert Williams, Laura Hodgson).

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- "Data ethics (of power) addresses the distribution of power and power relations in the Big Data Society and the conditions of their negotiation and distribution. Applied data ethics is concerned with making these power relations visible" (p. 10, quoted lecture slide 11).
- "BDSTIs are human-made spaces shaped by human imagination, compromise and domination of some interests over others" (p. 16).
- "Destiny Machines... are technological systems and processes designed to predict human behaviour based on the accumulation of personal data and then act on these predictions... they produce machine-readable people and shell out destinies on the other side of the production line" (p. 28).
- "We might all live in liquid forms of surveillance, but we do not all experience the reality of it" (p. 34).
- "A data ethics of power is not only about power, it is power. The power to raise issues, to define problems, to propose and create the solutions to the problems" (p. 34).
- "The change necessary to counter the violent and oppressive effects of intensive data collection... comes with the lived experience of being targeted and outside. It is a voice that is most often not the loudest one" (p. 36).

**(d) Connection to the week.** Provides the *power-analytic frame* the week needs. Hasselbalch installs three moves the lecture leans on: (i) data ethics is about *infrastructure*, not individual transactions; (ii) the relevant power is **distributed and asymmetric** — the Big Data Society both reproduces old inequalities (Browne) and creates new actors (Zuboff's tech CEOs); (iii) the question "who has a voice?" is the bridge to data ethical governance (chapter 2 onwards) and to Micheli et al.'s alternative governance models. This is the conceptual ground for the lecture's "data interest analysis" (slide 14) and for the case-work prompt to identify whose interests an organisation's data infrastructure serves.

### Micheli, M., Ponti, M., Craglia, M. & Berti Suman, A. (2020), "Emerging models of data governance in the age of datafication," *Big Data & Society* 7(2)

**(a) Core argument.** The dominant data governance regime — corporate platform extraction (Srnicek, Van Dijck, Zuboff) — is not the only one available. By scanning grey and academic literature on alternative practices, the article identifies **four emerging models** (DSP, DC, PDT, PDS) and analyses each across five dimensions (stakeholders, governance goals, value, mechanisms, reciprocity). The contribution is twofold: a **social-science-informed definition of data governance** as power relations over access/control/value (p. 3), and a typology that lets the policy and academic debate distinguish overlapping buzzwords ("data trust," "data sovereignty") that currently "tend to be used equivocally" (p. 4).

**(b) Method/empirical basis.** Speculative literature review with a snowballing strategy: 72 academic articles, 16 book chapters, 63 reports/policy documents, 22 project websites, plus inputs from a 2018 expert workshop (Micheli et al., 2018). The corpus was selected iteratively, in English, with a European focus; the four models are **Weberian ideal types** (p. 7) used to "synthetise phenomena that differ for the degree of affiliation to those traits."

**(c) Quotable passages.**
- "Data governance [is] the power relations between all the actors affected by, or having an effect on, the way data is accessed, controlled, shared and used, the various socio-technical arrangements set in place to generate value from data, and how such value is redistributed between actors" (p. 3).
- On DSPs: data is treated "as a market commodity with the aim of producing data-driven innovation... data subjects (and citizens in general) do not have a voice in this model; they are not included in the relation and are at best depicted as recipients" (pp. 7, 10).
- On DCs: "all parties are stakeholders and are equally affected and bound by the governing rules they discuss, negotiate and then agree upon" (citing Ho & Chuangt, 2019; p. 8). DCs are "in stark opposition to platform capitalism" (p. 10).
- On PDTs: "all data with a public interest component (even if collected by commercial entities) is part of a nation infrastructure, therefore the information it affords should be 'socialised' to produce value for citizens and society as a whole" (p. 9).
- On PDS: "A fully private ecosystem of intermediary services, even if allowing users to control their data, would leave the incumbency of addressing the power asymmetries of the current data landscape to the market" (p. 10).
- "We did not find a single model to be 'recommended' or 'promoted' for a fairer data landscape. Instead, a combination of all these models should be envisioned for a 'desirable future'" (p. 11).

**(d) Connection to the week.** Operationalises Hasselbalch's diagnostic frame as a *constructive* design space. Where Hasselbalch shows that BDSTI power is distributed and asymmetric, Micheli et al. show **what could be done at the governance level**: civic and public actors are already prototyping models in which data subjects have voice, public interest is generative of value, and reciprocity is built in. The article also gives the week its working vocabulary — DSP/DC/PDT/PDS — which the lecture (slides 20–25) operationalises with concrete examples (hospital data pool, mobility cooperative, environmental trust, smart-home sovereignty).

## 4. Lecture connections

Anders Koed Madsen frames the lecture (slide 3) around a single learning objective — analyse ethical challenges and opportunities in a data project — and then gives students two interlocking instruments for doing so: **data interest analysis** (slide 14) and the **four-model governance typology** (slides 20–25). The opening cases (slides 6–8) — Cambridge Analytica, Dieselgate, Uber's "God View" — concretise the diagnostic side: *what does ethical failure look like, and at what level of the BDSTI does it occur?* These map onto Hasselbalch's surveillance-industrial register (Cambridge Analytica = Zuboff's behavioural-prediction commodity; Uber God View = liquid surveillance inside a private firm; Dieselgate = "intentional algorithmic deception" of the very production-moment of regulatory data, slide 7).

Slide 5 plants Hasselbalch & Tranberg (2016, p. 9) as the *positive* programme — data ethics as the new "compass" — and slide 10 distils the eight markers of an ethical company: *go beyond legal compliance; follow the spirit of the legislation; listen to customers; transparency; data minimisation; privacy-aware culture; Privacy by Design; would I accept this as a consumer?* Slide 11 then upgrades the analysis to Hasselbalch (2021)'s *power* register: ethics is no longer just corporate hygiene but a question of "who is empowered, who is disempowered" by design.

Slides 14–15 give the *method*. The data interest analysis is multi-level (micro / meso / macro) and asks how interests are "empowered or disempowered by design"; this is a direct operationalisation of Hasselbalch's (2021) "asymmetric experience of data power" (pp. 32–34). Slide 15 grafts the EU AI HLEG's seven requirements (human agency, robustness, privacy and data governance, transparency, fairness, environmental and societal well-being, accountability) onto this and reframes them — slide 16 — as **"ethics as data innovation"**: each requirement is "a design choice (and a form of innovation)," echoing Micheli et al.'s claim (p. 4) that emerging governance models are "inventive practices that problematise current arrangements."

Slide 17 (Solid / Tim Berners-Lee) and slides 20–25 (the four-model walk-through) bring Micheli et al. into the room as the *constructive* repertoire. The lecture's healthcare DSP, mobility DC, environmental PDT and smart-home PDS are direct illustrations of Micheli et al. (2020, p. 6, Table 2). Slide 19 finally points at sites of European practice (dataethics.eu, european-alternatives.eu) — the same political project Hasselbalch (2021) names "the European third way" (pp. 21–22) — closing the loop between the week's critical and constructive halves.

## 5. Cross-paper synthesis

- **Agreement: data ethics is about power, not just rules.** Hasselbalch (2021) defines data ethics as "the distribution of power and power relations in the Big Data Society" (p. 10). Micheli et al. (2020) define data governance as "the power relations between all the actors affected by, or having an effect on, the way data is accessed, controlled, shared and used" (p. 3). The two papers, written separately, use almost the same wording: data ethics/governance is *power-relational*, not technical-legal.
- **Agreement: infrastructure is the unit of analysis.** Hasselbalch frames BDSTIs as the "naturalised background" of the Big Data Society (p. 14). Micheli et al. import "data infrastructure" from STS/CDS (Kitchin & Lauriault) as their analytical lens (p. 3). Both refuse the framing of data ethics as choices about individual transactions.
- **Disagreement on emphasis: critique vs. construction.** Hasselbalch's chapter is overwhelmingly *diagnostic* — surveillance capitalism, Destiny Machines, asymmetric experience, voicelessness. Micheli et al. are deliberately constructive: their stated motivation is to "respond to scholars and policy makers advocating for a reorientation of the scientific gaze, from the critique of the current data landscape to the possibilities of agency from the 'bottom up'" (p. 2). The two papers are complements, not rivals: Hasselbalch supplies the political stakes; Micheli et al. supply the institutional repertoire that those stakes can act on.
- **Extension: where does *voice* sit in the four models?** Hasselbalch's central charge is that "rarely do we hear the voices of the people who are subjugated to data power when solutions are proposed" (p. 34). Micheli et al.'s four models can be read as a ladder of voice: DSPs include no data-subject voice ("at best depicted as recipients," p. 10); PDS individualises voice into market choice; PDTs route voice through public-body trusteeship and "trust-building" mechanisms (p. 9); DCs are the most voice-rich, with "all parties... stakeholders... equally affected and bound by the governing rules" (p. 8). Reading the two papers together yields a normative criterion: a governance model is more *ethical-of-power* the more it expands voice into the spaces of negotiation (Hasselbalch, p. 36).
- **Tension: scaling vs. principle.** Micheli et al. note candidly that "DCs raise a lot of interest... [but] struggle to scale up and reach a critical mass of users" (p. 10) and that PDS "leaves the incumbency of addressing the power asymmetries... to the market" (p. 10). Hasselbalch would respond that scaling problems are themselves political — civil society and minority communities lack "the resources to participate and the competences to compete with the most powerful interests shaping the institutional politics of AISTIs and BDSTIs" (p. 37). The synthesis is that good governance design must include the conditions of participation, not only the legal form.

## 6. Likely exam questions

**Q1. Define "data ethics of power" and explain how it differs from data ethics framed as legal compliance.**
Hasselbalch (2021, p. 10) defines data ethics of power as addressing "the distribution of power and power relations in the Big Data Society." The contrast with compliance is structural: legal data protection regulates *transactions* (consent, purpose limitation, minimisation), whereas data ethics of power examines *infrastructures* — the spatial architecture of BDSTIs (p. 14) and the asymmetries it produces. Hasselbalch & Tranberg (2016) prefigure this in the lecture's "ethical company" criteria (slide 10), where ethics is explicitly "beyond legal compliance" and follows "the spirit of legislation, not just the rules." Micheli et al. (2020, p. 3) make the same move on the governance side: data governance is power relations among actors, not the rule-making subset of those relations. The pay-off for case work is that GDPR-compliance is necessary but not sufficient: a project can be lawful and still embed disempowering data interests.

**Q2. Walk through the four data governance models (Micheli et al., 2020) and identify, for each, where citizen voice enters.**
DSP: data holders join horizontal partnerships; data is a "market commodity"; data subjects "are not included in the relation and are at best depicted as recipients" (p. 10) — voice does not enter. DC: citizens "voluntarily pool their data... establishing a relationship of trust with the cooperative that manages data on their behalf" (p. 7); voice is **constitutive** — "all parties are stakeholders and are equally affected and bound by the governing rules" (p. 8). PDT: voice is mediated through public bodies as trustees, with "trust-building" mechanisms such as living labs and citizen consultations (p. 9); the model depends on whether the public body actually listens (failed Sidewalk Toronto example, p. 10). PDS: voice is **individualised** through personal data spaces (Digi.me, Meeco, MyData, slide 25) and Art. 20 GDPR data portability; the catch (p. 10) is that "the majority would not be capable, nor have the time" to exercise it. Read with Hasselbalch (2021, p. 34), the ladder runs DSP → PDS → PDT → DC.

**Q3. What does Hasselbalch mean by "Destiny Machines"? Why does the concept matter for data-driven management?**
Destiny Machines are "technological systems and processes designed to predict human behaviour based on the accumulation of personal data and then act on these predictions" (Hasselbalch, 2021, p. 28). The crucial property is that the machine is "fundamentally indifferent to the individual human being"; it operates only on the *data double* and on what Amoore calls the "data derivative" — what can be "imagined and inferred about who we might be" (p. 28). For data-driven management this is not abstract: the UK A-level algorithm (p. 33) and the Dutch SyRi system (p. 33) are Destiny Machines. The concept matters because it forces managers to ask whether their predictive system is *constituting fate* for the people inside it, and whether — per Hasselbalch & Tranberg (2016, lecture slide 10) — they would accept that prediction "as a consumer" or as a citizen.

**Q4. Why do Micheli et al. argue that no single governance model should be "recommended"?**
Their explicit conclusion is that "we did not find a single model to be 'recommended' or 'promoted' for a fairer data landscape. Instead, a combination of all these models should be envisioned" (p. 11). Each model fixes one asymmetry and creates another: DSPs scale economic value but exclude data subjects; PDS empowers individuals but offloads systemic asymmetry to the market (p. 10); PDTs socialise public-interest data but require legal frameworks and trust-building that "public bodies are (still) learning to do" (p. 10); DCs realise high reciprocity but "struggle to scale up" (p. 10). Hasselbalch (2021, p. 35) supports this pluralism: a holistic data ethical governance approach is needed because the power of the Big Data Society is "liquid" — concentrated *and* self-sustaining in cultures of use, design and imagination. The exam pay-off: pluralism is not a hedge; it is the analytic answer to a "liquid" power dynamic.

**Q5. How would you conduct a "data interest analysis" (lecture slide 14) for a case organisation?**
The analysis is multi-level (micro / meso / macro) and asks **whose interests are inscribed in the data design and who is empowered or disempowered**. Concretely: at the micro level, identify the data subjects and trace what categories the schema can and cannot represent (Hasselbalch's "data double," 2021, p. 28). At the meso level, identify intra-organisational interests — which department defined the metadata, who controls the dashboards, which Destiny Machines (predictive models) are deployed against whom. At the macro level, locate the organisation in the BDSTI: are its platforms part of the surveillance-industrial complex (Hasselbalch, 2021, p. 29)? Then map the project onto Micheli et al.'s (2020) typology: is it a DSP, a PDT, a DC, a PDS, or none? The output is a list of *interest claims* — whose value, whose voice, whose silence — and a candidate governance reform that would expand reciprocity (Micheli et al., p. 5).

**Q6. Hasselbalch (2021) emphasises voice as power. Where does that argument show up empirically, and what does it imply for governance design?**
Hasselbalch's strongest empirical move is to juxtapose the *technical* European debate — "stack-issues, application-requirements, decentralised technical infrastructures" (p. 36) — with personal accounts: Emma Holten on revenge porn, Laura Hodgson on the UK grading algorithm, Robert Williams on facial-recognition arrest. Her claim is that "we see nuances, we restate our problems and we find new solutions when social and personal experiences are voiced" (p. 36). The governance implication is concrete: simply *adding* affected voices to a panel "rarely made much of a difference" (p. 36); governance must build "spaces of negotiation" with the right conditions for participation. Micheli et al. (2020, p. 9) operationalise this in PDTs through living labs and consultations, and in DCs through cooperative co-decision (p. 8). A purely technical or legal fix will, in Hasselbalch's terms, perpetuate adiaphorisation — the abstraction of morality from the system (Bauman, in p. 30).

**Q7. Compare the Cambridge Analytica, Dieselgate and Uber "God View" cases (lecture slides 6–8) using both readings.**
Cambridge Analytica: behavioural prediction for political targeting; Hasselbalch's (2021) Destiny Machine in classic form, with the "data double" sold as electoral commodity (p. 28); Micheli et al. would diagnose absence of any reciprocity — data subjects had no voice, no Art. 20 portability could have prevented it. Dieselgate: data is *fabricated by* the device to game the regulatory production-moment; the BDSTI here is the regulatory-industrial infrastructure itself. Uber God View: liquid surveillance inside a corporate platform, classic surveillance-industrial complex (Hasselbalch, p. 29). Each case maps onto the same diagnosis — corporate concentration of data power, data subjects unable to "raise issues, define problems, or propose and create the solutions" (p. 34) — and each would be addressed differently across Micheli et al.'s models: Cambridge Analytica calls for PDS plus regulation; Dieselgate calls for a PDT-style audit infrastructure; God View calls for cooperative or trust-based governance of internal employee access.

**Q8. Hasselbalch & Tranberg (2016) frame data ethics as a "competitive advantage." Is that compatible with Hasselbalch's later "data ethics of power"? Use the lecture and Hasselbalch (2021).**
The lecture (slide 5) quotes Hasselbalch & Tranberg (2016, p. 9): "Data ethics must and will be a new compass to guide us." Slide 10's eight criteria — go beyond compliance, transparency, data minimisation, Privacy by Design, ethical reflection — are firm-level prescriptions. Hasselbalch (2021) does not contradict this; she *upgrades* the unit of analysis. Where the 2016 book argues that ethical data practice differentiates a firm in the market, the 2021 chapter argues that the market is itself an asymmetric BDSTI in which "the biggest risk lies in the unequal balance of power that the opaque data market creates between individuals and corporations" (p. 29, quoting their own 2016 book). Compatibility, then, is conditional: ethics-as-competitive-advantage is real *if* the firm is willing to act against the surveillance-capital logic that grants its competitors low-cost data extraction. Otherwise it collapses into what Micheli et al. (2020, p. 2) call a "technology of legitimation."

**Q9. Why do Micheli et al. (2020) treat "data trusts" as a buzzword, and how does this matter?**
Because the same word is used for very different things. In a DSP, a data trust is "a 'marketing tool' facilitating the responsible sharing of data" — a "repeatable framework of terms and mechanisms" between firms (p. 7, citing Hall & Pesenti, 2017). In a DC, it is a "bottom-up data trust" (Delacroix & Lawrence, 2019) that "act[s] in behalf of citizens' interests and preferences" (p. 11). In a PDT, it is a public body or independent trustee responsible for safeguarding citizens' data for public interest (p. 9). The same buzzword therefore carries different *rationalities of governance* (p. 11). For policy analysis and case work, this means resisting the term and asking, instead, *which model is being instantiated and on whose behalf?* — directly mirroring Flyverbom & Madsen's (Week 4) diagnosis of "big data" as a boundary object whose meaning shifts project by project.

**Q10. Hasselbalch closes by arguing that data ethics is "not only about power, it is power" (p. 34). What does this mean methodologically?**
Methodologically, it means that the *act* of analysis is itself a redistribution of power: pulling the BDSTI into the foreground (Star, in p. 17) restores the social narrative of an infrastructure and "makes change possible and the hidden social consequences manageable" (p. 17, citing Bowker et al., 2010). Empirically, the chapter mobilises this through disclosure: PRISM, the Snowden revelations, the EURODAC database, the SyRi system — each is a moment in which a BDSTI was forced to explain itself. For students, the methodological consequence is that a data ethics analysis is not a neutral audit; it is a participatory move into a "space of negotiation." Combined with Micheli et al.'s typology, it gives the case work a concrete deliverable: name the current governance model, name the silenced voices, name the alternative model that would re-include them, and name the legal or organisational mechanism that would scale it.

## 7. One-minute elevator answer

Week 7 reframes data ethics as a question of *power and voice* in socio-technical infrastructures, not legal compliance. Hasselbalch's "data ethics of power" treats the Big Data Society as a built environment — Big Data Socio-Technical Infrastructures — that quietly distributes who is empowered, who is surveilled, and who is silenced; her central methodological move is to ask who has the voice to raise the problems in the first place. Micheli et al. complement this critique with a constructive vocabulary: four governance models — Data Sharing Pools, Data Cooperatives, Public Data Trusts, Personal Data Sovereignty — that prototype alternatives to corporate-platform extraction. Hasselbalch & Tranberg add the firm-level argument that ethical practice is a competitive differentiator. For our case work the synthesis is concrete: do a data interest analysis, identify the silenced voices, and propose a governance model — DSP, DC, PDT, PDS or a hybrid — that would expand reciprocity and re-include them.
