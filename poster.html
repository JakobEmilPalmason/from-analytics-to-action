3Defining Arthouse

The cohort is the union of two complementary methods:

  • LLM scorer — GPT-class model rates each film 1–10 against a synthesized arthouse definition. Threshold: score ≥ 8. Catches auteur-driven films.
  • Rule-based filter — derived from team consensus on industry markers (festivals, distributors, art-cinema metadata). Catches industry-arthouse titles the LLM misses.

The definition was tuned through manual review of 50 random films, comparing method outputs against human judgement until both hit acceptable precision.

Cohort source split. LLM only: 3,516 (83.9%) · Rule only: 481 (11.5%) · Both: 194 (4.6%). The two methods catch different populations — they are complementary, not redundant.

5The Direction We Pursued · Good vs. Bad Provocation

Splitting 1,352 films with usable IMDb distributions on two axes — polarization (share of votes in 1–2 + 9–10) and mean rating — reveals two distinct controversy regimes. A single polarization score collapses them; the rating axis separates them.

Fig. 1 · Two kinds of arthouse controversy. Each dot is a cohort film. Quadrants split at cohort medians. The right side of the chart contains all "polarized" films — they split sharply on the rating axis with no middle.
Fig. 2 · Provocation lexicon. Among above-median-polar keywords, ranked by mean rating: top = productive, bottom = rejected. Same controversy magnitude, opposite reception.

Productive provocation

High polarization and high mean rating. A vocal refusing minority cannot kill the film. Productive lexicon: experimental, avant-garde, surrealism, essay, non-linear narrative, ambiguity, slow cinema. Anchor titles: Persona, Mulholland Drive, The Zone of Interest.

Rejected provocation

Same polarization profile, opposite verdict — heavy mass at 1–2 with a small defending tail. Rejected lexicon clusters around graphic-content keywords (the LLM's known false-positive mode). A loyal niche cannot save these films at audience scale.

Why this matters. Both regimes look identical on a single polarization score. They are not the same product. Polarization is a shape, not a number — and the shape predicts whether controversy converts.

6Recommendations & Limitations

Three operational uses for Publikum:

  1. Positioning. Market a polarized film as productively divisive only when its keywords land in the productive lexicon. With rejected keywords, controversy will not convert — sell on craft instead.
  2. Comparable titles. Comp-matching should include a polarization-shape filter, not just the arthouse label. A J-shape (love-heavy) film is comp-compatible with another J-shape film; pairing it with a U-shape mis-sets review-variance expectations.
  3. Greenlighting / acquisition. The productive lexicon is a checklist. Projects whose keywords cluster in the upper-right of Fig. 1 have, historically in this cohort, achieved both critical respect and engaged audiences. That is the bet an arthouse acquisition should optimize for.
One-line takeaway. Don't ask is this film controversial? — ask which kind of controversy does it carry? Productive provocation is what arthouse audiences pay for.

Limitations kept honest.

  • Polarization computed only on the 32% of films with ≥100 IMDb votes — small, recent, and non-Anglophone titles drop out.
  • Commercial ROI not analyzed (2.2% budget+revenue coverage, n=91).
  • Cinephile-discovery direction parked (MovieLens 1.5% coverage).
  • The lexicon is descriptive of past patterns, not predictive of new films.
  • Quadrant medians shift if the cohort definition shifts — recompute when re-scoping.

Did we provide business value? Yes — a positioning variable, a comp-shape filter, and a keyword checklist that historically separates productive from rejected controversy. All three plug directly into Publikum's existing decision workflows.
